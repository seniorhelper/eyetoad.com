Why Legal Is Different

Attorney Marketing Is Not General Business Marketing With a Gavel Icon

Four things make legal genuinely different, and agencies that miss them produce campaigns that look fine in a report and never fill a calendar.

1. The rules are binding. Colorado Rule of Professional Conduct 7.1 prohibits comparisons with other lawyers that cannot be factually substantiated and anything likely to create an unjustified expectation about results. That is not a style preference — it governs what can go on your website. Most generic marketing copy ("the best personal injury lawyer in Denver," "we win your case") would not survive review.

2. Specificity is the whole game. A page trying to rank for personal injury, criminal defense and estate planning simultaneously ranks for none of them. Search engines resolve pages to topics, and a page about everything resolves to nothing. This is also why solo practitioners routinely outrank multi-practice firms on the practice area they actually focus on.

3. The decision is made under stress. Someone arrested last night, or injured last week, is not comparison-shopping calmly. They are looking for signals that you are real, reachable and credible — recent reviews, a named attorney, a phone number that gets answered. Trust architecture is conversion work, not decoration.

4. Intake decides the outcome. Roughly 35% of prospective-client calls to law firms go unanswered, and 74% of legal clients prefer to make contact by phone. You can win every ranking on this page and still lose the case to whoever picked up. We measure intake before we recommend spending anything on traffic.

Colorado Rules of Professional Conduct

The Four Bar Rules That Actually Govern Your Website

Most marketing agencies have never read these. They apply to every page, every ad and every review response your firm publishes — and the violations we see most often are accidental, not aggressive.

Colo. RPC 7.1

Communications concerning a lawyer's services

A lawyer may not make a false or misleading communication. That includes any comparison with other lawyers' services that cannot be factually substantiated, and anything likely to create an unjustified expectation about results.

Avoid: "Denver's best injury lawyer" · "We win your case" · past verdicts presented in a way that implies similar future results.
Instead: verifiable specifics — years in practice, matters handled, published recognitions, named certifications.
Colo. RPC 7.1, cmt. [7]

Contingency fee language

The comment is explicit: a statement such as "no recovery, no fee" is misleading unless it also discloses that the client may be liable for costs of the lawsuit. This is one of the most frequently missed items on personal injury websites.

Incomplete: "No fee unless we win."
Compliant: "No attorney fee unless we recover. Client may be responsible for case costs." — placed with the claim, not buried in a footer.
Colo. RPC 7.2

Advertising & paying for recommendations

Advertising is permitted across any medium, but a lawyer may not give anything of value to a person for recommending their services, with narrow exceptions such as the reasonable cost of advertising and the usual charges of a qualified lawyer referral service. Your communications must also identify the office address.

Practical effect: paying an agency for advertising is fine. Paying anyone per referred client is not. CBA Formal Ethics Opinion 122 addresses where internet marketing programs fall on that line.
Colo. RPC 7.3 & 7.4

Solicitation and "specialist" claims

Rule 7.3 prohibits soliciting a prospective client through real-time electronic contact. Rule 7.4 restricts describing yourself as a specialist or certified to lawyers certified by an approved or ABA-accredited organization — which must be named in the communication.

Risky: a chat widget that initiates live outreach to identified prospects · "Specializing in personal injury" with no certification.
Safer: "Our practice focuses on personal injury" · "Practice areas include…"
