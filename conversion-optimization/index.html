The biggest lever, and it is getting bigger

The Device That Sends You the Most Visitors Converts Worst

Everyone assumes mobile has caught up by now. It has not — the gap has widened. And for a local service business there is a second twist that makes your own numbers look worse than they actually are.

The gap grew. WordStream's benchmarks put the mobile-desktop performance gap at 38% in 2024 and 42% in 2026. Mobile optimisation has not kept pace with mobile traffic growth — which means the opportunity is larger this year than it was last year, not smaller.

The twist that applies to you specifically

Most of that benchmark data comes from e-commerce, where a conversion means a completed checkout. Your customers do not check out. They call.

And mobile users convert differently in a way that matters enormously here: a person on a phone is far more likely to tap a phone number or fill a three-field form, and far less likely to complete a long form or a multi-step flow. If your site only counts form submissions as conversions, your mobile numbers are not just low — they are wrong, because the calls never entered the count.

That is the first thing we check, and it is frequently the whole finding. We have seen businesses conclude their site was failing when the truth was that two thirds of its conversions were arriving on a phone line nobody had connected to the analytics.

📌 Short answer

For a local service business, 3–5% on contact forms and consultation requests is a reasonable target. The all-industry median in 2026 sits near 2.35%, while the top 10% of sites reach about 11.45%. But before comparing yourself to anything: if you aren't tracking phone calls, you're probably seeing only about 60% of your actual conversions — average lead conversion of 2.9% breaks down to roughly 1.7% from forms and 1.2% from calls. Fix the measurement first, then the site.

Why this is the cheapest work available to you

It is also the half of the job most agencies skip. We build it into every engagement, including the search work we are best known for, because rankings that do not produce calls are a bill rather than a result.

Every other marketing lever costs more to pull harder. More rankings take months. More ads cost more money. Conversion optimization is the one place where the same traffic produces more revenue — you've already paid for these visitors.

The arithmetic is unsentimental. A thousand visitors a month at 1% is ten leads. The same thousand at 3% is thirty. Nothing upstream changed; nobody spent more.

The gap between a median site and a top-decile site has grown roughly fivefold since 2022. Being average is more expensive than it used to be.

And there's a sequencing argument that most agencies skip because it costs them work: sending more traffic to a page that doesn't convert raises the cost of every future visit. If you're about to increase spend on monthly SEO or ads, this is the work that should come first.

The 40% of your leads you probably can't see

Before optimizing anything, this one is worth an hour of your time, because it invalidates every number you're currently working from.

Across a broad set of industries, average lead conversion runs about 2.9%. That figure decomposes into roughly 1.7% from forms and 1.2% from phone calls.

Which means if you're only counting form submissions — as most analytics setups do by default — you're seeing a little over half your conversions. For a local service business, where people call rather than fill out forms, the phone share is often higher still.

The six places leads actually leak

Five of the six are fixable on the site you already have. The sixth — a page whose structure makes it slow no matter what you strip out — is the one case where rebuilding is cheaper than patching, and we will tell you plainly which situation you are in.

Ordered by how often they turn out to be the real problem, not by how obvious they look.

01

Untracked phone calls

A measurement failure, not a design one

Listed first because it's the only one that makes every other diagnosis unreliable. Roughly 40% of lead conversions arrive by phone. If those aren't recorded, your conversion rate is fiction and so is every decision based on it.

Do this first: get call tracking in place before you change a single thing on the site.
02

Mobile experience

Where most of your traffic already is

Mobile carries 65–75% of local search traffic and converts at roughly half the desktop rate — 1.8–2.8% against 3.2–4.0%. Some of that gap is inherent to the device. Most of it, on most sites, is fixable.

Do this: open your site on your own phone, on cellular, not office wifi. Try to call yourself in under five seconds.
03

Time to usable

Not "page speed score" — time to usable

The metric that matters isn't a number in a testing tool, it's how long before a real person on a real connection can actually do something. Largest Contentful Paint under 2.5 seconds is the working threshold, and images are almost always the culprit.

Do this: this one helps rankings too, since page experience is a signal Google measures directly.
04

Unclear offer and next step

You have about five seconds

A visitor decides quickly whether you can solve their problem. If your headline describes your company rather than their situation, and the next action isn't obvious without scrolling, a competitor gets the call. Clear beats clever, reliably.

Do this: show your homepage to someone outside your industry for five seconds, then ask what you do and what they'd click.
05

Trust proof in the wrong place

Position matters more than quantity

Reviews, credentials and real photos parked at the bottom of the page do almost nothing. The same proof placed immediately beside the call to action — where hesitation actually happens — is one of the highest-impact changes available on most pages.

Do this: move your star rating and one real review to sit directly next to your primary button.
06

Form friction

Every field has a cost

Each additional required field reduces submissions. Ask for what you genuinely need to make the first contact — usually name, one contact method, and a sentence about the problem. Qualifying questions belong in the conversation, not the gate.

Do this: count your required fields. If it's more than four, ask what each one earns you.

✅ Realistic expectations

Quick structural wins — speed, call-to-action clarity, form simplification — commonly produce lift within weeks rather than months. That's the main reason to do this before scaling traffic.

The honest caveat: low-traffic sites take longer to prove a change worked, because you need enough conversions for a difference to be distinguishable from normal variation. Under a few hundred conversions a month, treat improvements as directional rather than statistically settled.

The 20-minute self-audit

Run this yourself before hiring anyone, including us. Every item is checkable without a tool.

  1. Open your site on your phone, on cellular.Not office wifi — that hides the problem. Count the seconds until you could actually tap something useful.
  2. Try to call yourself in under five seconds.Is the number visible without scrolling, and does tapping it start a call rather than select text?
  3. Read only your first headline.Does it say what you do and for whom, or does it say something about excellence and commitment?
  4. Count your required form fields.More than four needs a justification for each one.
  5. Submit your own form.Does it confirm receipt on screen? Does the email actually arrive? Check spam. This fails more often than anyone expects.
  6. Look at what sits next to your main button.Is there any proof there — a rating, a review, a credential — or is the proof somewhere near the footer?
  7. Check whether your calls are recorded anywhere.If the answer is "we just kind of know," you don't have a baseline.
  8. Look at your site with the images not yet loaded.Throttle your connection or just watch it load cold. What appears first should be the point, not a hero image.
  9. Ask someone outside your industry what you do.Five seconds on the homepage, then take it away. Their answer is your actual message.
  10. Find your slowest page with real traffic.In Analytics, sort your top landing pages by engagement. The worst performer with meaningful volume is where to start.

How an engagement runs

Measurement first. Everything else is guesswork until step one is done.

Step 1

Measure properly, including calls

Establish the real conversion rate before touching anything. For a local service business that means tracking phone calls as well as forms, because a baseline built on form fills alone understates performance and misdirects everything after it.

Step 2

Walk the path a real buyer takes

On a phone, on cellular, from a cold start. Time to usable, clarity of offer, whether the number is tappable, how many fields the form demands, and whether anything is asked before value is given.

Step 3

Rank the fixes by impact against effort

Not every problem is worth solving first. Cheap high-impact items go first — speed, message clarity, form simplification — because early lift funds patience for the slower structural work.

Step 4

Change deliberately, in a sequence you can attribute

Eight changes in one week and a lift tells you something worked, not what. Where volume allows, test. Where it doesn't, change one meaningful thing at a time and give it long enough to read.

Step 5

Re-measure against the baseline, then repeat

Calls, forms and booked work against week one — not impressions, not sessions. Keep what moved the number, revert what didn't, and treat it as continuous rather than a project with an end date.

Conversion when the visitor came from an AI — or is one

This is changing what a landing page has to do, and almost nobody has adjusted for it yet.

Two practical consequences follow. Visitors who were pre-qualified by an assistant arrive expecting the page to confirm what they were told, not to re-sell them — which is a different job for the copy. And because a growing share of them arrive outside office hours, something on the page has to be able to answer them. Being named by the assistant is its own discipline; converting the visitor it sends is this one.

Traffic arriving from AI assistants converts at roughly 3.49% against 2.86% from traditional organic — about 22% higher. The reason is straightforward: those visitors already had their options narrowed before they clicked. An assistant named you, they read a summary, and they came anyway.

Two consequences worth acting on. First, these visitors are further along and correspondingly less patient. They've already done the comparison step. A slow page or a vague next step wastes a visitor who was closer to buying than anyone arriving from a broad search.

Second — and this is the part that's genuinely new — some of the visitors are software. Agentic assistants that research and shortlist vendors on a person's behalf need machine-readable contact paths: a phone number in the markup rather than baked into an image, hours and service area stated as text, and a form that doesn't depend on JavaScript to exist. The same work that helps a hurried human helps an agent.
