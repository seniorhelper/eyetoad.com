The problem

Your numbers are scattered across tools. So is your Monday.

Ask most owners how last month went and you get a pause, then a tour of browser tabs. Leads sit in the ad accounts and the call tracker. Jobs sit in the field software. Revenue sits in QuickBooks. Reviews sit on Google. Each one has its own date picker and its own idea of what counts as a lead, so the full picture only exists after someone stitches it together by hand, and by then the week it describes is already gone.

What we connect

It reads from the tools you already pay for.

No new software for your team to learn. We connect to the systems where the work already happens and pull the figures on a schedule, so the screen is current when you open it.

  • Google Analytics 4Visits, form fills and the conversions you care about, by source.
  • Google Search ConsoleThe searches you show up for, clicks and average position.
  • Google Business ProfileCalls, direction requests and website clicks through the Business Profile Performance API.
  • Call trackingCalls by source, answered versus missed, and first-time callers, from platforms such as CallRail that offer an API.
  • Google Ads and Meta AdsSpend, clicks, leads and cost per lead, side by side instead of in two logins.
  • CRMs and field softwareHubSpot, ServiceTitan, Jobber and similar: estimates, booked jobs, close rate and average ticket.
  • QuickBooks OnlineRevenue, invoices and what is still owed, so the money side sits next to the marketing side.
  • SpreadsheetsGoogle Sheets, Excel or a CSV your team already keeps. If it lives in a sheet, it can live on the dashboard.

The honest part: not every platform hands its data over freely. Google reviews each request for Business Profile API access, the Google Ads API needs an approved developer token, and some CRMs and field-service platforms open their API only on certain plans or after their own approval process. We check each of your tools during discovery, before you pay for a build, and tell you which numbers can flow in on their own and which need a simple weekly upload.

How it works

From scattered logins to one screen you trust.

  1. Discovery

    We sit down with you and pick the 10 to 12 numbers that actually run the business, then give each one a person responsible for it and a target. That limit follows guidance published for New Jersey CPAs, and it keeps the screen readable.

  2. Build

    We connect your accounts, settle the definitions (what counts as a lead, when a job counts as booked) and design the layout around how you check things, on a phone, a laptop or a TV in the shop.

  3. Launch

    You and your team get logins, a short walkthrough and a weekly email digest. We check the numbers against your source systems before anyone relies on them.

  4. Care

    Platforms change their APIs, passwords rotate and fields get renamed. The care plan keeps the connections working and the numbers right, and adds new views as your questions change.

Source for the KPI limit: Ann Means, "KPI Dashboards for Small Business: Measure It to Manage It", New Jersey CPA magazine (NJCPA), 2020.

Design principles

Built to be read in a minute, not admired.

A dashboard earns its keep when someone glances at it and knows what to do next. These rules come from people who have studied how we read charts for a living.

One screen, at a glance

Stephen Few, author of Information Dashboard Design, describes a dashboard as the most important information needed for a goal, arranged on a single screen so it can be monitored at a glance. If you have to scroll to find out whether the week is going well, it is a report, not a dashboard.

Length and position over pie slices

The Nielsen Norman Group notes that people compare lengths and positions accurately and judge angles poorly, so bars and lines beat pie charts for almost every comparison. That is why the demo above uses bars.

No 3D, no decoration

The same research warns that adding a third dimension distorts shapes and alignment and makes charts slower to read. Flat, labeled and boring is the point.

Color flags, it doesn't measure

Nielsen Norman Group advises against using color to show quantity. We use it to flag what needs attention, and always pair it with a word like "Up" or "Watch" so it works for color-blind readers too.

Source: Page Laubheimer, "Dashboards: Making Charts and Graphs Easier to Understand", Nielsen Norman Group, 2017. Stephen Few, Information Dashboard Design, 2006.
