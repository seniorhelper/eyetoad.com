What is dental SEO?

Dental SEO is the work of getting a practice found and chosen by patients searching for a dentist. It covers the Google map pack that appears for dentist near me, the procedure pages on your own site that match what patients actually type, the review profile they read before calling, and increasingly the AI-generated answers that summarize local options before any list of links appears. The goal is not traffic. It is a fuller schedule of the appointments you actually want.

Three places production disappears

A practice can lose the same patient three different ways

Most owners assume they have a marketing problem. Usually they have one of these three, and the fix for each is completely different.

01

Never found

You are not in the three listings a patient sees, so you were never in the running. Being fourth is not a slightly worse version of third, because nobody expands the list. Fixing this is profile work, review flow and real procedure pages, and it is usually the fastest thing to move.

02

Found, not chosen

They saw you and picked someone else. Almost always the review profile, the photos, or a site that looks like it was built in 2016. A patient cannot evaluate your clinical skill from a phone. They can only evaluate whether the practice looks current and cared for.

03

Chosen, then lost

They called during lunch and nobody picked up. They tried to book at 9pm and the only option was a form. They came once and never scheduled the treatment plan. This is the most expensive category because you already paid to acquire them.

Spending on the first when your problem is the third is how practices conclude that marketing does not work. If you have traffic and an empty schedule, the honest diagnosis is in traffic but no calls.

Three patients, one search box

They are not all looking for the same thing

Building the site for only one of them is the most common structural mistake in dental marketing.

Emergency

"Tooth pain, dentist open now"

In pain, deciding in under a minute, choosing from the map pack. Wins on hours, proximity, a tappable number and someone answering. Your philosophy of care does nothing here. A buried phone number loses them outright.

Insurance

"Dentist that takes my plan"

Methodical and slightly anxious about cost. Searching by plan name and coverage. Wins on a page that answers the insurance question plainly instead of making them call to find out. Vagueness here reads as a hidden bill.

Elective

"Clear aligners vs veneers cost"

Researching a purchase, not a treatment. Weeks of comparison, several practices, real price sensitivity and real aspiration. Wins on procedure pages, honest ranges and before-and-after proof. This is the highest-value patient and the one most sites ignore entirely.

A practice built only for the first never fills its elective chairs. Built only for the third, the phone never rings today. You need all three, on separate pages, doing separate jobs.

Local demand

What actually drives dental search in a metro like ours

Four patterns show up every year. All four are predictable, which means all four can be planned for rather than reacted to.

The benefits cliff

Unused insurance benefits expire at year end, producing a reliable Q4 surge from patients who suddenly want treatment scheduled before December 31. Practices that message it in October catch it; practices that remember in December do not.

January resets

New plans, new deductibles, new employers. A large share of "dentist that takes my insurance" searches cluster in the first six weeks of the year, and they are won on a page that answers coverage plainly.

Elective research season

Aligners, whitening and veneers get researched months ahead of weddings, graduations and reunions. That research happens well before the season, which is exactly when most practices go quiet.

The radius problem

Nobody drives across a metro for a cleaning, so a city is really a dozen separate local contests. Winning your own few square miles beats ranking vaguely everywhere — more in how a metro splits up and seasonal search demand.
