Why Is My Competitor Outranking Me?

Seven causes, ranked by how often they are actually it — each one checkable yourself in about ten minutes, without buying a tool. Most people find their answer in the first three, and the first two are free to fix.

18 min read Eye To Ad Media · Denver Updated Sep 2026

The short answer

In roughly half the cases we look at, the competitor is not actually outranking you — you are checking from a location, browser or account that produces a different result than your customers see. That one is free to fix and takes four minutes.

When the gap is real, the most common causes in order are: they are physically closer, their page answers the question while yours describes your company, and their page is considerably older than yours. Backlinks and technical problems are further down the list than almost anyone expects.

It is a genuinely horrible feeling. You search the thing you do, in the place you do it, and there they are — above you. A company you know is not better than you, in a position you can see, taking calls that had a decent chance of being yours.

The instinct at that point is to go and buy something. Links, usually, or a rewrite, or a different agency. That instinct is nearly always premature, because most people skip the diagnosis entirely and jump to a treatment for a condition they have not confirmed they have.

So this is the diagnostic, in the order we actually run it. Seven causes, ranked by how often they turn out to be the real one, each with a check you can do yourself in about ten minutes without buying a tool. Work down the list in order. Most people find their answer in the first three, and the first two cost nothing at all.

1. You are checking it wrong

This is the most common finding by a distance, and it is the one nobody wants to hear because it implies the problem was never real.

Search results are personalised. Your location, your search history, whether you are signed in, and whether you have visited that site before all shape what you see. When you google yourself from your own office, on your own laptop, signed into your own account, you are seeing a result almost nobody else gets.

There are two distinct versions of this error, and they run in opposite directions.

The false alarm

You see a competitor above you, panic, and start spending. But you searched from a spot closer to their office than yours, or Google has learned you visit their site (perhaps because you keep checking on them), and it has obliged by ranking them higher for you specifically.

The false comfort

The more expensive version. You search from your office, see yourself at the top, and conclude everything is fine. Your office is the one place on earth where Google is most confident you are the right answer. Your customers are not standing in your office. We wrote about how sharply local rankings vary by position in our piece on local SEO across the Denver metro — the short version is that your position can differ dramatically a few neighbourhoods away.

The four-minute check. Open a private or incognito window so you are not signed in. Then search the term as a customer would phrase it — the service, not your business name. If your business name is in the search, you will always come first and you will learn precisely nothing.

For local terms, go further: use Google Maps and re-centre the map on a different part of your service area before searching. That shows you what somebody standing there actually sees. We built a free tool that does this in one click if you would rather not fiddle with coordinates.

Run that before anything else on this list. In a meaningful share of cases the panic resolves here, and the correct response is to do nothing and go back to work.

2. They are simply closer

If the search has local intent — and most searches for a service do — then physical distance between the searcher and the business is one of the primary inputs. Google says so directly: relevance, distance and prominence.

Two businesses with equally good profiles, equally good reviews and equally good websites will not rank equally for a searcher standing between them. The closer one usually wins, and there is no amount of optimization that changes where your building is.

This is worth accepting cleanly rather than fighting, because fighting it is expensive. We have watched businesses spend months and real money convinced they were doing something wrong with keywords or photos, when the honest answer was that their competitor had a branch nearer to more of the searches happening.

What to do instead

  • Check whether you are losing everywhere or only far away. If you dominate within three miles and vanish at ten, that is geography, not a failure of your marketing.
  • Compete where the map pack does not decide it. Longer, more considered searches produce fewer map results and more organic ones, and those are winnable at distance.
  • Build prominence hard. Relevance and prominence can partially offset distance. They will not let you win a map pack across a metro, but at the margins they decide ties — and there are more ties than you would think.

3. Their page answers the question. Yours describes your company.

Now we reach the most common cause that is genuinely within your control, and the one where the fix is fastest relative to its impact.

Open the competitor page that is beating you. Then open yours. Read both as though you were the person who typed that search. In a large proportion of cases the difference is immediately obvious: their page answers the question that was asked, and yours introduces the company.

Somebody searching "how much does a furnace replacement cost" wants a number, or at least a range and the reasons it varies. A page that opens with "Welcome to our family-owned business serving the area since 1998" has not answered them. It has answered a different question that nobody asked.

Google's systems are built around satisfying the intent behind a query, and first-hand experience — original data, real examples, actual specifics — has become the single largest content differentiator. A page written by somebody who has genuinely done the thing reads differently from a paraphrase, and increasingly it ranks differently too.

The ten-minute check. Take your page and the competitor's. Write down the question the searcher was asking. Then mark how far down each page that question gets answered. If theirs answers in the first paragraph and yours answers in paragraph nine, or not at all, you have found your problem and it is fixable this week.

4. Their page is older than yours, and that is most of it

This is the cause people resist hardest, because there is nothing to buy that fixes it.

Roughly 73% of pages ranking in Google's top ten are more than three years old. Not because Google prefers old things, but because a three-year-old page has had three years of accumulating links, being updated, earning engagement, and building the history that a page published last month has no way to fake.

If the page above you went live in 2022 and yours went live in March, a substantial part of the gap is calendar rather than quality. That is a genuinely important thing to know, because it changes what a reasonable expectation looks like. Publishing a page and checking its rank six weeks later tells you almost nothing.

It also explains a pattern that frustrates owners constantly: the competitor's page is worse than yours, plainly worse, and still above you. Sometimes that is exactly right. It has three years of head start, and you are watching compound interest from the outside.

What actually helps

  • Stop deleting and republishing. Every time a page is replaced at a new URL, its history resets. Update in place and keep the address.
  • Update the pages you already have rather than adding new ones next to them. Content updated within 90 days correlates with roughly twice the ranking stability of content left alone.
  • Be patient in a specific way. Six to twelve months for a competitive term is normal. If someone promised faster, that is worth remembering when it does not happen.

5. They cover the topic. You cover the page.

Here is a structural difference that is invisible until you go looking for it.

You have one page about a service. They have one page about that service plus eight or ten supporting pieces around it — the costs, the common problems, the comparison against the alternative, the questions people ask before buying. The site with a cluster consistently outranks the site with a single excellent standalone page on the same subject, and published analysis puts the advantage at roughly 30% more organic traffic and over three times the rate of being cited by AI answer engines.

The reason is intuitive once stated. A cluster demonstrates that the site covers the subject rather than mentions it. It also generates internal links pointing at the main page from genuinely related content, which is the cleanest signal of importance you can send about your own site.

This is slower than the fixes above — it is a real project, not an afternoon. But it is also the thing that tends to separate businesses that plateau from those that keep climbing, and it is the logic behind how we structure an ongoing SEO engagement rather than treating pages as one-off deliverables.

The check: search your main service term and look at how many pages on the winning site are relevant to it. If they have twelve and you have one, you are not competing page against page. You are competing one page against a body of work.

6. Better links, not more links

Links still matter, and they matter most for competitive commercial terms where content quality alone cannot separate the contenders. What has changed is which links count.

Topical relevance now matters roughly four times more than raw quantity. Ten links from sites genuinely connected to your industry or your city will outperform a hundred from directories nobody reads. This is good news for small businesses, because ten relevant links is achievable by a person with a phone and some patience, whereas a hundred of anything is a budget item.

There is a related finding worth more attention than it gets: brand search volume — the number of people searching your business name directly — has emerged as the strongest off-page authority predictor, ahead of the domain metrics most tools report. People searching for you by name is a signal that is very hard to fake and very obviously meaningful.

Which has an implication that sounds unlike SEO advice: the sponsorship, the local press mention, the podcast appearance, the thing that makes people in your area know your name — those feed the same machine. Offline reputation and search visibility are less separable than they used to be.

What to do

  • Stop buying links. Beyond being against Google's guidelines, the cheap ones are irrelevant, which now means close to worthless.
  • Go local and go relevant. Suppliers, trade associations, chambers, local news, charities you genuinely support, partners you genuinely work with.
  • Look at who links to them. Whoever links to your competitor is demonstrably willing to link to a business like yours. That is the warmest list you will ever get.

7. Something technical is broken

Last, and rarer than the industry implies — though when it is the cause, it is usually the whole cause.

Only around 56% of pages pass all three Core Web Vitals together, and passing one or two is not enough when the competition is close. If your page is slow on mobile, unstable while it loads, or sluggish to respond to a tap, that is a genuine competitive disadvantage in a tight race.

Worse and rarer: a page blocked from indexing, a stray canonical tag pointing somewhere else, a redirect chain, or content that only appears after JavaScript runs. These are invisible to a human looking at the page and decisive to a crawler.

The check: run your page through PageSpeed Insights on mobile and compare against the published thresholds. Then open Search Console, use the URL inspection tool on the page, and confirm Google can actually index it. Both are free and together take about ten minutes.

The order matters more than the effort

One more thing worth saying before the diagnostic, because it is the mistake that costs the most money and it is not on the list of seven.

Almost everyone works this list backwards. They start at seven, because technical problems feel like the kind of thing an expert should find. Or they start at six, because links are what SEO sounds like it should involve. Both are near the bottom for a reason, and starting there means spending real money before confirming there is anything to fix.

What we check first

  • Is the gap even real, checked properly
  • Is it geography, and therefore fixed
  • Does the page answer the actual question
  • How old is each page

What people ask us for first

  • A backlink campaign
  • A technical audit
  • A full site rebuild
  • More content, immediately

The left column is free or nearly free and resolves most cases. The right column is where the invoices live. We are not suggesting anyone is being deliberately misled — it is genuinely easier to sell a defined project than to tell somebody their problem was the browser they checked from. But the sequencing is worth defending, because it is your money.

A note on AI answers changing the picture

One complication worth naming, because it is increasingly the real explanation and none of the seven causes cover it. AI-generated summaries now appear on a substantial share of searches, and where they do, they can reduce clicks to the top-ranked page significantly — published estimates run as high as a 60% reduction in click-through for the first organic result on affected queries.

Which produces a genuinely confusing situation for a business owner: your ranking has not changed, your traffic has fallen, and nothing on the diagnostic list applies. Before assuming a competitor took something from you, check whether your impressions held steady while clicks dropped. That pattern points at the answer box above the results rather than at any rival, and it requires a different response entirely — which we covered in how AI is changing local search and address directly through generative engine optimization.

Work out which one it is

Seven checks in the order we run them. Tick what you have actually verified — not what you assume — and it will point at the most likely cause.

  1. Have you checked in incognito, signed out, searching the service rather than your business name?

  2. Is their address closer than yours to where most of your customers search from?

  3. Does your page answer the searcher's question in the first paragraph?

  4. How old is your page compared with theirs?

  5. How many pages does each site have on this topic?

  6. Do they have links from sources genuinely connected to your industry or city?

  7. Does your page pass Core Web Vitals on mobile, and can Google index it?

Answer all seven to see the most likely cause.

What to do with the answer

The reason the order matters is that the work is wildly different at each level, and doing the expensive one first is how budgets disappear.

If it is cause one, you are finished. Close the tab, stop worrying, and check properly next time before anyone spends anything.

If it is cause two, adjust the target rather than the effort. Compete on the searches geography does not decide, and accept the ones it does. Businesses that fight geography lose slowly and expensively.

If it is three, you have found the best available trade in local search: a rewrite of one page that can be live this week and can move a real term. Start there regardless of what else is also true.

If it is four, five or six, you are looking at a programme rather than a fix. That is fine, but it should be entered deliberately, with a realistic horizon — six to twelve months on a competitive term — and a way of measuring progress that does not depend on refreshing a search result every morning.

If it is seven, fix it immediately and then re-run this list, because a technical fault masks everything beneath it.

The uncomfortable possibility

There is an eighth answer that does not fit on a chart: sometimes the competitor is genuinely doing better work. Their page is more useful, their reviews are more numerous and more recent, their site is faster, and they have been at it consistently for longer.

That is not a pleasant conclusion, but it is an actionable one, and it is considerably more useful than assuming they found a trick. In competitive markets there is usually no trick. There is just somebody who has been doing the unglamorous version of this for three years while everyone else looked for a shortcut.

We see this constantly doing Denver SEO in categories where several agencies are active — roofing, dental, legal, home services. The businesses at the top are rarely there because of one clever thing. They are there because they kept showing up. That is genuinely replicable, which is the good news buried in an answer nobody wants.
