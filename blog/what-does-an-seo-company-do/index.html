What Does an SEO Company Actually Do? A Month-by-Month Breakdown

Not the sales-page version. What an SEO company does in month one, month three and month twelve, what changes when you pay more, and the questions that separate real work from an invoice.

By Zach WennstedtPublished September 28, 202613 min readReviewed for accuracy Sept 2026

Key takeaways

  • An SEO company works seven areas — technical foundation, content, local and maps, authority, AI visibility, conversion and reporting — and a good one sequences them: fix, then build, then compound.
  • Month one is mostly diagnosis and repair. Months two to six are building. After that the work gets wider, not different.
  • More budget mostly buys more of the same work done wider and faster, not a secret eighth area.
  • The report you should demand is calls and booked work, not rankings and impressions.
  • In 2026 'SEO' includes Google Maps and AI answers, or it isn't finished.

If you've asked an SEO company what they actually do and received a list — "10 backlinks, 4 blog posts, 2 schema implementations per month" — you've been handed a menu, not an answer. If you've asked and been told "it's proprietary," you've been handed a shrug. Neither tells you what happens to your business after you sign. This is the version I'd give a friend.

Direct answer: an SEO company diagnoses and repairs a website's technical foundation, builds pages that match what customers actually search, completes and grows the Google Business Profile for map visibility, earns mentions and links from credible sources, structures the site so AI systems can cite it, improves how visitors convert into calls, and reports on results in revenue terms. The work is sequenced — fix first, then build, then widen — and budget changes depth and speed rather than the list.

Month one: the company finds out what's actually wrong

A serious SEO company spends the first weeks looking, not doing. The audit covers four things, and each one produces a to-do list.

  • Technical. Can Google crawl and index the site? Is it fast on a phone? Are there noindex tags, redirect chains, duplicate pages, broken links, missing or wrong structured data? These are the fast, cheap fixes with outsized effect, and they go first because nothing else works until they're done.
  • Keywords and pages. What do customers type, what do you rank for now, and where is the gap? Which existing pages are close to ranking and just need help? Which money pages don't exist yet?
  • Local. The Google Business Profile: categories, services, hours, photos, reviews, Q&A, consistency with the site and the directories. For most local businesses this is the fastest lever available, and it's usually half-finished.
  • Conversion. When someone lands on the site, what does it ask them to do, and does that work? A ranking that doesn't ring is a failure.

By the end of month one the company should have fixed the technical problems, finished the Business Profile, and handed you a plan that names the pages it will build and why. If you got a report full of "impressions up 12%" instead, ask what was fixed.

Months two and three: building the pages that should have existed

This is where most of the visible work happens. The company builds or rewrites the money pages — one per real service, per real place, written to answer the question the customer typed. Not "Welcome to our website." Not five city pages with a swapped name. A page about emergency plumbing in Lakewood that says what it costs, how fast you come, and what happens when you arrive, written by someone who knows.

Alongside the pages: internal links so Google understands which pages matter; titles and descriptions that earn the click; structured data that matches the visible content; and the first authority work — directory listings, a press release, a supplier or association mention. If the company owns properties for this (we own a national directory and a press-distribution platform), this is when they get used.

What you should see by month three: the map pack moving, if you're local. Rankings for less competitive phrases starting to appear. Search Console showing pages that didn't exist before getting impressions. Not a flood of calls yet — but the first ones that clearly came from search.

Months four to six: compounding starts

By now the foundation is fixed and the core pages exist. The work turns to depth and authority: more pages for more services and neighborhoods, refreshing what's underperforming, earning more real mentions, adding the AI-citation layer (fact blocks, entity signals, direct answers, machine-readable summaries), and testing the conversion path on the traffic that's arriving. Reviews should be growing systematically, because the company set up a way to ask.

This is the stretch where competitive phrases begin to move. It's also the stretch where a bad SEO company gets found out: if the report in month five looks exactly like the report in month two, with the same "we're building authority" language and no calls, the work isn't happening.

Months seven to twelve: wider, not different

After six months the seven areas don't change. The work gets wider and faster: more markets, more services, more surfaces (Maps, AI answers, video), ongoing conversion testing, and strategy changes when search changes — which it will, several times a year. A good company tells you when it changes course and why.

A fair expectation at twelve months for a local business in a competitive metro: owning the map pack for core terms, page-one organic for most services, AI answers mentioning you for some, and a phone that rings measurably more than it did. Not a guarantee — nobody honest offers one — but the pattern we see when the work is done.

What changes when you pay more

This is the question owners are most afraid to ask, so here's the plain version: more budget mostly buys more of the same work, done wider and faster. More pages built per month. More markets. More authority-building. Faster iteration on conversion. A $500 campaign and a $3,000 campaign work the same seven areas; the $3,000 one works them across three cities instead of one, and gets there in half the time.

Larger campaigns do add a few things smaller ones can't carry — press distribution at scale, multi-market builds, agentic systems, custom conversion tools. But there is no secret eighth area that unlocks at a price. Any company that implies there is should be asked to name it.

We put this on a sliding scale on our What You Get page, and we don't publish a monthly deliverable count — partly because some of the method is proprietary, mostly because search changes constantly and a fixed list would force us to do work that stopped mattering. That's the honest trade: you get the areas and the depth, and a strategy that changes when it should.

The report you should demand

Rankings are a means. Impressions are a vanity metric. The report that matters answers one question: is the phone ringing more, and from where? That means call tracking, form tracking, Business Profile actions (calls, direction requests, website clicks), and — if the company is serious — which pages and which searches produced them. In plain English, on one screen.

Ask three questions of any monthly report: What did you do? What moved? What changes next month because of it? If the report can't answer all three, it's decoration.

What an SEO company should not do

  • Guarantee a ranking. Google's own guidance says no one can. A guarantee is a sales tactic, not a service.
  • Buy links. Cheap, fast, and the surest way to get a site penalized. Real mentions take work; that's why they count.
  • Publish AI filler. Generic, confident, wrong text is the fastest way to lose Google's trust and your customer's. AI is fine for research and speed; a person who knows the trade has to read every page.
  • Keep your accounts. You should own the Business Profile, the analytics, the domain and the site. If leaving means losing them, don't start.
  • Ignore Maps and AI. In 2026, "SEO" that stops at the ten blue links is a partial job. The map pack and the AI answer each decide separately whether you exist.
