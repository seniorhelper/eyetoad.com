NFC Marketing for Small Business: The Complete Tap-to-Connect Guide

The chip has been in every phone for years. Almost nobody local uses it. Here is what it actually does, where to put it, what it really costs, and the situations where a plain paper QR code beats it outright.

Quick answer

NFC marketing means putting a small wireless chip on a physical surface so a customer can tap a phone and instantly open a web page. No app. No camera. No typing. The chip costs well under a dollar, holds a web address, and works on roughly 94% of smartphones in circulation.

Most local businesses use it for one job above all others: collecting Google reviews at the moment a customer is happiest. Beyond that, the common uses are digital business cards, restaurant menus, booking pages, window decals for after-hours inquiries, and vehicle decals for service trades.

Here is a strange gap in small business marketing. The technology sitting in almost every customer's pocket costs you about fifty cents to use. Yet walk down any commercial street in Denver and you will struggle to find a single business using it.

Meanwhile the ones who do are quietly collecting reviews at the counter. They hand over cards that open a booking page in one second. And they put decals in the window that turn a closed-shop visit into a lead instead of a lost customer.

So this guide covers the whole thing properly. Not the sales-brochure version. We will cover how the technology works, the eight placements that genuinely earn their keep, the review rules that carry real legal exposure, honest cost maths, and — importantly — the situations where NFC is simply the wrong tool.

What NFC actually is

NFC stands for Near Field Communication. Strip away the jargon and it is very simple. A tiny chip sits inside a sticker, a card, or a plastic stand. That chip stores a small amount of data — usually just a web address.

When a phone comes within about four centimetres, it powers the chip wirelessly and reads what is stored. Then it offers to open the link. The whole thing takes about a second.

Two details matter more than people expect. First, the chip has no battery. It draws its power from the phone's field, which is why a sticker can sit on a window for three years and still work. Second, the customer needs no app. Reading NFC is built into the operating system on both Android and iPhone.

The standard is maintained by the NFC Forum, the industry body that also governs the data format used, called NDEF. That matters for one practical reason: because it is a genuine open standard rather than a proprietary product, a tag you buy from any supplier will work with any phone. There is no lock-in.

Worth knowing

NFC is the same technology behind tap-to-pay. When a customer taps a card at your terminal, that is NFC. So the behavior is already familiar — you are not teaching anyone anything new, which is a large part of why it works.

There is a speed argument too. Contactless payment transactions complete in roughly two to three seconds, against eight to twelve seconds for a chip insert. That difference sounds trivial written down. In practice it is the difference between a customer doing the thing and a customer deciding it is not worth the bother.

NFC vs QR, honestly compared

Most NFC guides are written by companies that sell NFC products. So they tend to skip this comparison, or stack it. Let us not do that, because the honest answer is more useful.

Getting a customer from a physical surface to your web page

Steps and approximate time, from the customer's side

NFC tap

~1 sec

Two actions, no aiming

  • Hold phone near tag
  • Tap the notification

QR code

~5–8 sec

Four actions, needs light and aim

  • Unlock phone
  • Open camera
  • Frame and hold steady
  • Tap the banner

Step counts observed from standard iOS and Android behavior. Timings are approximate and vary with lighting, distance and device age. QR remains the more reliable option at distance or on older hardware.

Now the honest part. QR beats NFC in several real situations.

  • Distance. A QR code works from across a room. NFC needs contact. So a poster on a wall or a billboard is QR territory, full stop.
  • Cost at volume. A QR code is ink. Printing ten thousand flyers with a QR costs nothing extra. Ten thousand NFC tags do not.
  • Universal compatibility. Every phone with a camera reads QR. A small share of older phones need extra steps for NFC.
  • Visibility. People can see a QR code and understand it is scannable. An NFC tag is invisible unless you label it.

That last point is the one businesses get wrong most often. An unlabelled NFC tag is a tag nobody uses. It needs a printed prompt — "tap here", the little wave symbol, something.

The practical answer

Use both on the same piece. Put the NFC tag inside the card or stand, and print a QR code on the face of it. Customers who know what to do get the fast path. Everyone else gets the reliable one. This costs almost nothing and removes the entire compatibility argument.

A note on the window decal, because it is the most underrated of the eight. Every closed storefront leaks customers. Someone walks up, finds the door locked, and leaves. That person had intent and you lost them.

A decal at eye height that opens your booking form converts a portion of those walk-ups into leads. It costs about fifteen dollars. And it works at two in the morning while you sleep, which is more than most marketing can claim.

The vehicle decal has a similar logic. A van parked at a job site is seen by every neighbor on the street. Most of them will not write down a phone number. Some of them will tap a decal on the door.

The killer use case: Google reviews

If you only ever do one thing with NFC, do this. Reviews are the highest-leverage asset a local business owns, and the gap between "customer is happy" and "customer leaves a review" is almost entirely friction.

Think about what you normally ask someone to do. Unlock their phone. Open Google. Type your business name. Scroll to find the reviews section. Tap write a review. Find the stars. That is six steps standing between a happy customer and a five-star review.

A tag collapses that to two. Tap, then write.

Two of those bars deserve a second look.

The jump from 29% to 41% in a single year is enormous for a behavioral statistic. More people are not just glancing at reviews. They are treating them as a required step.

Then there is the recency figure. 74% weight reviews from the last three months more heavily. That reframes the whole problem. A business with 200 reviews where the newest is from 2023 looks worse to a modern buyer than a business with 40 reviews where the newest is from last week.

So reviews are not a one-time campaign. They are a flow. And a tag on the counter is the cheapest way to keep that flow running without anyone remembering to do anything.

The ranking side matters too. Reviews contribute roughly a tenth of local ranking weight, which is why they show up in every serious analysis of what drives Google Maps positions. They also feed the AI systems now summarising local options, which we covered in how AI Overviews are changing local search.

Timing beats everything

Ask within 24 to 48 hours of the service, while the experience is fresh. Better still, ask in the moment — at the counter, in the van, at the door. A tag makes the in-the-moment ask possible, which is exactly why it outperforms the follow-up email nobody opens.

The rules you must not break

This is the section most NFC guides leave out, and it is the one that can actually cost you money. Two separate sets of rules apply when you solicit reviews.

Google's rules: no review gating

Review gating means screening customers before you show them the review link. The classic version asks "how did we do?" first, then routes happy customers to Google and unhappy ones to a private complaint form.

Google prohibits this. It is a policy violation, and profiles caught doing it can have reviews removed or face further action. So do not build a tag that asks a qualifying question first. Point it straight at the review form for everyone.

Yes, that means some unhappy customers will leave honest negative reviews. That is the deal. And the data suggests it matters less than owners fear — a profile with only perfect scores reads as suspicious, while a business that responds well to criticism often builds more trust than one with a spotless record.

The FTC's rules: no incentives

The Federal Trade Commission's Consumer Review Rule carries real teeth. On 22 December 2025 the FTC sent its first-ever warning letters under the rule to ten companies. Violations carry civil penalties of up to $53,088 each.

So do not offer anything of value in exchange for a review. No discount for reviewing. No free coffee. No prize draw entry. No "leave us five stars and get 10% off."

You may ask. You may make asking easy. You may not pay.

Do not do these

Never screen customers by sentiment before showing the review link. Never offer discounts, freebies or entries in exchange for reviews. Never write reviews for customers, even with permission. Never point a tag at a form that filters out negative feedback before it reaches Google.

Each of these is either a Google policy violation, an FTC exposure, or both. The upside is not worth it — a suspended profile costs more than any number of reviews is worth.

The safe version is simple. The tag opens the Google review form. Everyone who taps it sees the same thing. You ask sincerely, at a good moment, and then you accept whatever comes.

Where costs actually climb is design and volume. Five hundred printed cards with custom artwork is a real project. A single tag on the counter is an afternoon.

A word on subscriptions

Plenty of vendors sell NFC cards with a mandatory monthly fee attached. Sometimes that buys genuine value — hosted profile pages, analytics, the ability to update a destination centrally. Often it does not. Before signing up, ask what happens to your card if you stop paying. If the answer is "it stops working," you are renting, not buying.

Where NFC fails

Honest limitations, because pretending they do not exist is how projects die in a drawer.

Metal kills it

A standard tag stuck to metal usually will not read at all. The metal interferes with the field the phone uses to power the chip. So vehicles, appliances, machinery and metal signage all need on-metal tags with a ferrite shielding layer. They cost a dollar or two more. Buy them, or the project fails on day one.

Older iPhones need a nudge

Every iPhone from the XR onward reads tags in the background automatically. The iPhone 7 through X can read tags, but the user has to open the NFC Tag Reader from Control Center first. Almost nobody knows that exists. This is the single strongest argument for printing a QR code alongside every tag.

No tracking without a redirect

The tag is passive. It reports nothing. If you write your Google review link directly to the tag, you will never know whether anyone tapped it. Always write a short link instead, then read the tap data from the link service.

Weather is unkind

Denver is a hard environment for adhesive. Sun through a south-facing window, then freeze-thaw cycles overnight. Cheap laminated stickers curl and yellow within a season. Outdoor placements need weatherproof or epoxy tags, and even those want checking twice a year.

Invisible tags do nothing

This is the most common failure and it has nothing to do with the hardware. A tag with no printed prompt beside it gets zero taps. People do not tap surfaces speculatively. Label it, every time.

When to skip NFC entirely

Choose a printed QR code instead if the surface is a poster, a billboard, a vehicle wrap seen from the road, a newspaper ad, a mailer at scale, or anything a customer views from more than arm's length. NFC has one job — close contact — and outside that range it is the wrong tool.

Setting it up in an afternoon

None of this needs a developer. Here is the whole process for a review tag, which is the one to build first.

  1. Get your Google review link. Open your Google Business Profile, choose "Ask for reviews", and copy the short link Google generates. That link opens the review form directly with the stars ready. Google's own Business Profile help center walks through it if you cannot find the option.
  2. Wrap it in a redirect. Create a short link pointing at that review URL. Write the short link to the tag, never the raw Google URL. This is the step almost everyone skips, and it is the one that lets you change the destination later without replacing a single tag.
  3. Write the tag. Open a free NFC writing app, choose a URL record, paste the short link, and hold the tag flat against the back of the phone until it confirms. It takes about three seconds.
  4. Test on both platforms. Tap it with an Android and an iPhone. Test through any case or holder you intend to use, because a thick case can push you past the four-centimetre range.
  5. Lock it if the public can reach it. Anything a stranger can touch should be locked so it cannot be overwritten. Locking is permanent, so only lock tags whose destination will never change. Staff-only tags stay rewritable.
  6. Place it where the moment happens. The counter after payment. The folder handed over at the end of a job. The table after the plates are cleared. Placement is the whole game.

Then say something. A tag on a counter that nobody mentions gets used occasionally. A tag that a staff member points at while saying "if you've got ten seconds, it really helps us" gets used constantly. The technology removes friction. It does not replace the ask.

How to measure it

Marketing you cannot measure quietly becomes marketing you cannot defend. So set up the measurement before you deploy, not after.

The short-link service gives you taps. That is your first number, and it tells you whether the placement works at all. Zero taps after two weeks means the tag is invisible, badly placed, or unlabelled — not that NFC does not work.

Next, add UTM parameters to the destination URL so those visits show up in analytics as their own source rather than getting dumped into direct traffic. Something like ?utm_source=nfc&utm_medium=counter&utm_campaign=reviews is plenty. Use a different medium value per placement and you will learn which surfaces earn their keep.

For review tags specifically, the honest measure is simpler: count reviews per month before and after. If you were getting three a month and you are now getting nine, the tag works. No attribution model required.

One caution. Taps are not conversions. A tap that opens a slow, badly built page is a wasted tap. If your traffic is arriving and then leaving, the problem is downstream, and that is a conversion problem rather than an NFC problem — the same pattern we described in why your website gets traffic but no calls.

Where this fits in the bigger picture

NFC is a delivery mechanism. It moves someone from a physical surface to a digital destination faster than anything else available. But it does not create demand, and it does not fix a weak destination.

So think of it as the last inch of a longer path. Someone finds you through local search, or an AI assistant recommendation, or a neighbor. Then they arrive. Then a tag closes the gap between arriving and acting.
