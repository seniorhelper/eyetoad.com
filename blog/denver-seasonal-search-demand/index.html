When Denver Customers Actually Search: A Season-by-Season Demand Map

Hail in June. Furnaces in October. Patios in March. Denver demand does not drift with the seasons — it spikes. And because rankings take months to move, most businesses start marketing exactly one season too late.

Quick answer

Denver demand is unusually seasonal because of elevation, Hail Alley and a short frost-free window. Roofing and auto glass spike within hours of a hailstorm, concentrated in a six-week window from about 15 May to 25 June. HVAC has two peaks — cooling in summer, heating around the 7 October average first freeze. Outdoor trades run March to October.

The practical consequence: since search rankings take three to six months to move, you have to start marketing one to two seasons before the money arrives. Most businesses market during their busy season, which is backwards.

Ask a Denver roofer when their year happens and they will not say summer. They will say "after the storm."

That is the difference between Denver and most markets. Demand here is not a gentle seasonal curve. It is a set of switches, thrown by weather, and most of them get thrown in a handful of weeks.

Which creates an awkward problem. Search visibility is slow — three to six months to move meaningfully. Demand is fast. So by the time a business feels the busy season, the window to prepare for it closed months ago.

This guide maps when Denver demand actually arrives, why the weather drives it, and — more usefully — when you need to start working to catch it.

Why Denver is unusually seasonal

Three things compound here that do not compound in most cities.

Elevation. Denver sits at 5,280 feet. That matters more than it sounds. Atmospheric freezing levels sit much closer to the ground on the high plains than they do at sea level, so hail has far less distance in which to melt before it lands. The same storm that drops rain in Kansas City drops ice here.

Hail Alley. Colorado, Nebraska and Wyoming form the corridor meteorologists call Hail Alley. The National Weather Service puts the area at seven to nine hail days a year, and the Rocky Mountain Insurance Information Association describes the Front Range as seeing the highest frequency of large hail in North America.

A short frost-free window. Denver's average first freeze is 7 October, with Climate Central's 1991–2020 figure at 4 October. Average first snowfall follows on 18 October. That compresses outdoor work, roofing, painting, landscaping and concrete into a defined stretch, and it throws the heating switch on a fairly reliable schedule.

Put those together and you get a market where demand does not gently rise. It arrives.

Worth knowing

Denver's first freeze has landed as early as 8 September — twice, in 1962 and 2020 — and as late as 15 November 1944. So "average" is a planning tool, not a promise. Build your marketing to be ready two to three weeks before the average, not on it.

Hail: the defining Denver event

If you serve homeowners in any capacity, hail is the single most important date on your calendar. And the season is far narrower than most people assume.

Separately, NOAA Storm Events Database analysis indicates roughly 60–70% of significant Front Range hail damage occurs in a six-week window from about 15 May to 25 June, and Colorado State University's Climate Center places the peak of severe hail days in early to mid June.

Eighty-eight percent of confirmed hail days fall in three months. Half fall in one.

The financial scale is worth stating too. The costliest Colorado hail storm on record hit on 8 May 2017 and produced $2.3 billion in insured damage across the Denver metro. Colorado ranked second in the United States for hail claims between 2013 and 2015, with 182,591 — behind only Texas, a state roughly five times its size.

And the intensity appears to be climbing. Reports of baseball-sized hail in Colorado rose from 12 in 2019 to 34 in 2023, with softball-sized reports reaching 13 in the same year, according to NWS Denver.

Why this changes marketing timing completely

A hailstorm creates demand in hours, not weeks. Homeowners search that evening. So the businesses that capture a storm are the ones who were already ranking, already had reviews, and already answered the phone — before the storm existed.

You cannot buy your way into position after a June hailstorm. Everyone else is bidding on the same words on the same afternoon, and organic rankings will not move for months. The work has to be done by April.

The backwards problem

Here is the core mismatch, and it explains most wasted seasonal marketing budget in this city.

Demand is fast. Visibility is slow.

Search rankings typically take three to six months to move meaningfully, which we covered in detail in what to expect in the first 90 days. Google Business Profile improvements and review building take a month or two to register. Paid ads are close to immediate but cost the most exactly when everyone wants them.

Now overlay that on a June demand spike. If a roofer starts SEO in June, the work lands in September — three months after the season ended. They will have paid for a full campaign and captured almost none of the demand it was built for.

Run it the other way and the arithmetic works. Start in December or January, and by the time hail season opens in mid-May, the rankings, the content and the review volume are all in place.

The rule

Subtract six months from your peak for organic search work. Three to four months for reviews and Google Business Profile. One month for paid. Those are your start dates — not the month the phone gets busy.

Two things usually surprise people the first time they use it.

The first is how early the build season starts. If your peak is June, you are working in December. That feels wrong until you remember the rankings are what take the time, not the storm.

The second is that most industries have two useful windows, not one. There is the peak, where demand is highest and competition is fiercest, and there is the shoulder, where fewer competitors are bidding and the people searching are planning rather than panicking.

Industry playbooks

The specifics matter, so here is how the pattern actually plays out by trade.

Roofing

The most weather-dependent business in Denver. Demand does not build — it detonates. A single storm can produce more inquiries in 48 hours than the preceding three months combined.

That has a strategic consequence most roofers underrate. Because the surge is short and enormous, your share of it is determined entirely by preparation. Ranking, review volume, and phone answering capacity on the day are what decide the year.

Start by: December to February for organic work. April at the very latest for reviews and Google Business Profile. Have paid campaigns built, paused, and ready to switch on the hour a storm hits — that readiness is worth more than any bid strategy.

The often-missed opportunity: September and October. Homeowners who deferred a repair after a June storm start worrying about winter. Competition has collapsed. Leads are materially cheaper. Our roofing SEO work leans on this window heavily.

HVAC

Two peaks, which is unusual and useful. Cooling demand rises June through August. Heating demand spikes around the first freeze — with 7 October as the average, though it has arrived as early as 8 September.

The valuable months are the shoulders: April–May and August–September. That is when the tune-up market lives, when competition is thinner, and when you can book maintenance work that turns into replacement work later.

Start by: February for the cooling peak, June for the heating peak. Both are four months of lead time on a three-to-six-month process, which is the minimum that works.

The often-missed opportunity: the week before the first hard freeze. Furnace searches spike the first genuinely cold night of the year. Anyone with a paid campaign already live that week captures an outsized share. HVAC SEO is largely a game of being early.

Landscaping, concrete, painting and outdoor trades

These follow the frost-free window closely. Planning searches begin in March, quoting runs April through June, and work continues until roughly the first freeze in early October.

Start by: October or November of the previous year. That feels absurdly early, and it is exactly why so few competitors do it. Publishing project content through winter means it has ranked by the time anyone starts searching in March.

Auto glass and collision

Hail-driven, same as roofing, with the same instant-surge dynamic. Windshields also crack in winter from thermal shock — a warm defroster against a frozen windshield — so there is a genuine secondary winter peak that most operators ignore entirely.

Med spas, salons and aesthetics

Two reliable peaks: pre-summer, roughly March through May, and pre-holiday in November and December. January brings a resolution bump that fades quickly.

Start by: December for the spring peak, August for the holiday one.

Legal, dental and medical

Comparatively flat, which is its own advantage — steady demand means steady returns from organic work rather than a scramble.

Smaller patterns still exist and are worth catching. Dental demand rises in November and December as insurance benefits expire unused. Family law inquiries tend to lift in January. Personal injury tracks driving conditions, so it rises during icy months.

Emergency services — plumbing, restoration, locksmiths

A floor of demand year-round, with weather-driven spikes on top. Burst pipes during January freezes. Water damage after June storms. Sump pump failures during spring melt.

For these businesses the map-pack position matters more than almost anything else, because emergency searches convert immediately and rarely involve comparison shopping. We covered the mechanics in the service business guide to Google Maps.

Retail and hospitality

Tourism peaks in summer and ski season. Holiday retail runs from roughly late October. Restaurant patio demand tracks weather almost day by day, which makes Google Business Profile posts and photos unusually valuable — they are the fastest signal you can update.

What to do in your slow season

The most expensive mistake a seasonal Denver business makes is going quiet when the phone does.

It feels responsible. Revenue is down, so costs come down with it. But cutting marketing in the slow season means cutting it during exactly the months when the work has time to take effect — and then paying peak prices during the months when it cannot.

The fix is not to spend the same amount all year. It is to change the mix.

Three specific slow-season jobs are worth naming, because they compound better than anything else.

Collect reviews from the season you just finished. You have a list of happy customers and time to contact them. Reviews take weeks to accumulate and they weight heavily toward recency, so building them now means arriving at your next peak looking active rather than dormant. The method is in how to get more Google reviews without being annoying.

Publish the content that will rank by your peak. Three to six months of lead time means the article you publish in December is working in June. Write about what customers asked you last season — those questions are your keyword research, already done.

Photograph your best work. You finished it, you have time, and photos feed your Google Business Profile, your site and your ads for the next two years. Almost nobody does this, and it is free.

One thing not to do in the slow season

Do not rebuild your website in the four weeks before your peak. A rebuild often causes a temporary ranking dip while the new site is recrawled, and there is no worse month for that dip than the one your revenue depends on. Rebuild at the start of your slow season, not the end.

Spending in a rhythm, not a flat line

Most local businesses set a monthly marketing budget in January and leave it alone. In a market this seasonal, that is close to guaranteed inefficiency.

Consider what a flat budget actually does. In February it overspends on paid ads nobody is searching for. In June it underspends during the only weeks that genuinely matter. The same annual total, allocated with the season, would produce meaningfully more work.

A rhythm that works for most weather-driven Denver trades:

  • Slow season: roughly 60% of your average monthly spend, almost entirely on organic work — content, technical fixes, reviews, photography.
  • Build season: around 100%, still mostly organic, with paid campaigns being constructed and tested at low budget.
  • Pre-peak: 120–150%, shifting decisively into paid as demand approaches.
  • Peak: as much as your capacity can actually service — and not a dollar more.
  • Shoulder: back to around 80%, keeping paid running at lower bids while leads are cheap.

That last constraint matters more than the numbers. There is no value in generating leads you cannot answer. If you are booked four weeks out in June, additional spend does not produce revenue — it produces frustrated callers who leave a review about how you never rang back.

A note on the years the weather does not cooperate

Some years there is barely any hail. Some Octobers stay warm into November. Planning around averages means occasionally planning around a season that does not arrive.

Two things make that survivable.

First, organic work does not expire. A quiet hail season does not delete your rankings — it defers the return by a year. The content still sits there, still ranks, still earns when the weather turns. This is the fundamental difference between organic and paid: unspent ad budget is gone, while an unused ranking waits.

Second, diversify what you rank for. A roofer who ranks only for storm damage has a bad year when the storms do not come. One who also ranks for inspections, maintenance, gutters and repairs has a floor underneath them.

The businesses that survive quiet years in this market are almost always the ones whose visibility was broad enough that a single missing weather event did not empty the calendar.
