How to Get Cited by ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google AI

AI answers don't rank pages — they quote sources they can read, verify and trust. Here is what makes a business quotable, what we did to get our own companies named by AI assistants, and a checklist you can run today.

By Zach WennstedtPublished September 28, 202614 min readReviewed for accuracy Sept 2026

Key takeaways

  • AI systems cite sources; they don't rank them. Being quotable is a different job from ranking.
  • Five signals do most of the work: a plain-text statement of who/what/where, facts that match everywhere, structured data that agrees with the page, direct answers, and mentions on sources AI already reads.
  • Nobody can promise a citation. Anyone who does is selling you something.
  • The same work that makes you quotable makes you rank better on Google and Maps — it isn't a separate budget line, or shouldn't be.
  • Test it yourself: ask ChatGPT for a business like yours in your city. If you're not in the answer, you now know what to fix.

This agency's most recent SEO lead came from ChatGPT. Not from Google, not from a referral — a business owner asked an AI assistant who to talk to about SEO in Denver, and the assistant named us. That's the new front door, and most businesses don't know it exists. Here's what we did to get through it, applied first to our own companies and then to clients.

Direct answer: to get cited by ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews or AI Mode, a business has to be quotable — a clear plain-text statement of what it is, does and where; facts that match across its website, Google Business Profile and directories; structured data that agrees with the visible page; content that answers questions directly; and mentions on sources AI systems already read. No one can guarantee a citation, but these signals make one likely.

How AI answers actually choose who to mention

Google ranks pages. It scores them, orders them, shows ten. An AI answer does something different: it retrieves a set of sources that seem relevant, reads them, and writes an answer that cites the ones it found clear and trustworthy. The technical term is retrieval-augmented generation, and the practical consequence is this: the AI isn't looking for the best-ranked page. It's looking for the clearest, most verifiable source it can quote without embarrassing itself.

That changes what wins. A beautiful homepage with the services listed in an image, a slogan for a headline and the address in the footer is invisible to a retrieval system. A plain page that says "Eye To Ad Media is a Denver SEO agency at 1001 Bannock St, founded in 2012, that does X, Y and Z, typically for $500–$3,000 a month" is exactly the kind of sentence an AI can lift into an answer — and it will, if other sources agree.

Which brings up the second thing AI systems do: they cross-check. If your site says one thing, your Google Business Profile says another and a directory says a third, the system can't verify you, and it moves on to a business it can.

The five signals that make a business quotable

1. Say who, what and where — in text, up front

The first screen of your homepage and every key service page should contain a sentence a machine could quote: the business name, what it does, where it does it, and for whom. Not a slogan. Not "Welcome." A direct statement. We put a "direct answer" capsule near the top of every page for exactly this reason — you're reading one on this article. Google's own AI documentation says the same thing in different words: make sure the content you want surfaced is in the text of the page, not locked in images, PDFs or scripts.

2. Make the facts match everywhere

Name, address, phone, hours, services, founding year, the founder's name — identical on the site, the Google Business Profile, the major directories, your social profiles and any press. Retrieval systems treat agreement as evidence. Disagreement is a reason to skip you. This is unglamorous work and it's the most common reason a business that should be cited isn't.

3. Structured data that agrees with the page

Schema markup (Organization, LocalBusiness, Service, FAQPage, Article, Person) gives a machine a labeled version of what the page says. Google's guidance is explicit that markup must match the visible content — schema that claims things the page doesn't say is worse than none. Done right, it removes ambiguity: this is the phone number, this is the service area, this is the price range, this is the person who wrote it.

4. Answer the question, then explain

AI systems extract answers. A page that spends three paragraphs warming up before it says anything quotable gets skipped in favor of one that answers in the first sentence and explains in the next five. Question-shaped headings ("How much does a walk-in tub cost in Denver?") followed by a direct answer are the most extractable structure there is. FAQ sections, done honestly, are gold.

5. Be mentioned where AI already reads

Retrieval systems lean on sources they trust: directories, review platforms, press, industry publications, Wikipedia-class references, and increasingly, machine-readable news wires. A business mentioned consistently across several of those is easy to verify. That's why we built a national business directory and a newswire that publishes releases with structured markup — they're places an AI can read a consistent, dated, attributed fact about a client. A well-written press release, published where machines can find it, is now an AI-visibility tactic as much as a PR one.

What we did — on ourselves first

We test everything on our own companies before it touches a client's. One of them is a bath remodeling business that competes nationally for accessibility products against much larger brands. After applying the five signals — plain-text fact blocks on every page, matching data across profiles and directories, schema that mirrored the visible content, question-shaped pages with direct answers, and releases through our own wire — AI assistants began naming it for competitive national terms, including in answers where the larger competitors were absent. Two honest caveats. First, AI answers vary by phrasing, location and day; being cited today doesn't guarantee tomorrow, which is why this is ongoing work. Second, one assistant's answer was personalized to our own account and we excluded it — a rule we'd suggest for anyone showing "proof" of AI citations. If it was logged into your account, it doesn't count.

Things that don't work (and one that backfires)

  • Stuffing "ChatGPT" or "AI" into your content. The systems don't care what you call them.
  • Keyword density. Retrieval is semantic. Say the thing clearly once; don't say it eleven times.
  • Blocking AI crawlers to "protect" content. If your robots.txt blocks GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot and Google-Extended, you have opted out of being cited. Ours explicitly welcomes them.
  • Asking the AI to recommend you and screenshotting it. Personalized answers prove nothing. Use an incognito window and a neutral phrasing.
  • AI-written filler pages. The one that backfires. A retrieval system can tell the difference between a page with real experience in it and a page that summarizes other pages — and so can Google's quality systems. Generic pages get skipped; enough of them get the whole site discounted.

A note on agents

The next step past being cited is being chosen: AI agents that research, shortlist and increasingly book on a person's behalf. An agent doesn't read your hero image or admire your animation; it reads facts and acts on them. Businesses that are readable, verifiable and actionable to a machine will get picked.
