How to Respond to Google Reviews: Good, Bad and Fake

By Zachary Wennstedt, founderPublished October 2, 202612 minute read

Why the reply matters more than the review

A review is written once by one customer. The reply beneath it is read by every prospect who opens your profile for as long as the review exists, and it is read by Google. That makes the owner reply one of the few pieces of marketing copy a business publishes that sits directly inside the place customers decide whether to call.

The short version: reply to every review within two business days, in a human voice, with one specific detail from the review and one fact about how you work. Never argue, never template, never ignore. The reply is for the next customer, not the last one.

Google says it plainly in its guidance for business owners: responding to reviews shows that you value customers and their feedback, and high-quality, positive reviews can improve your business's visibility. The visibility part is the piece most owners miss. Reviews and the way a business engages with them feed the prominence signal in local ranking, one of the three factors Google names alongside relevance and distance. A profile with 80 reviews and 80 replies reads as an active, attended business. A profile with 80 reviews and no replies reads as a business that stopped paying attention in 2022.

There is a second reader now. AI assistants quote reviews when they explain why they recommended a business, and they read owner replies as part of the record. A calm, specific reply to a bad review is evidence of how you handle problems, and that is exactly what an assistant is looking for when a customer asks "who can I trust with this." We cover how to earn the reviews in the first place in our guide to getting more Google reviews; this article is about what to do once they arrive.

The rules that apply to every reply

  • Reply within two business days. Faster for negative reviews. The clock matters because the reviewer is watching for a reaction and so is everyone reading the thread that week.
  • Use the reviewer's name and one detail from what they wrote. That is the whole difference between a reply and a template. "Thanks for the kind words" could be pasted anywhere. "Glad the second-floor bathroom is finally quiet, Dan" could only have been written to Dan.
  • Add one fact about how you work. Your hours, your guarantee, who to call, what you do when something goes wrong. The reply is the only review-adjacent copy you control; use it to answer the next customer's question.
  • Never argue. Never reveal private details. Even when the reviewer is wrong. Especially when the reviewer is wrong. The reader is deciding about you, not about them, and health, legal and financial businesses have confidentiality obligations that a reply can breach in one sentence.
  • Keep it short. Two to four sentences for a positive review, four to six for a negative one. Long replies read as defensive.
  • Sign it. A first name and a role. People trust a person more than a brand voice.

Five-star reviews: say something back

Most businesses either ignore positive reviews or paste the same thank-you under each one. Both are missed opportunities, because the five-star reply is the easiest place to tell the next customer something true about your business without sounding like an ad.

Structure: thank them by name, repeat the specific thing they praised in your own words, add the fact about how you work that explains why it happened, and close warmly. The customer said you showed up on time; the reply mentions that you text an arrival window. The customer said the office was calm; the reply mentions that you schedule extra time for first visits. You are not bragging. You are confirming that the good experience was a system, not luck.

One caution: do not ask for referrals, upsell, or drop a promo code in a review reply. It reads as transactional and Google's content policies for business replies prohibit promotional content.

One-star reviews: the response that wins the reader

A one-star review is not a crisis. An unanswered or badly answered one-star review is. Prospects expect every business to have a few; what they watch is the response. Here is the structure that works, in order.

  1. Acknowledge without conceding facts you dispute. "I am sorry the experience fell short of what you expected" is honest even when you think the customer is wrong. "I am sorry we made a mistake" is only for when you did.
  2. Name the specific issue in neutral words. Show you read it. "The appointment started 40 minutes late" or "the quote came in higher than the estimate." Do not explain yet.
  3. State what you did or will do. One sentence. "We called this morning and left a message." "We have changed how we schedule the first slot of the day."
  4. Move it offline with a named person. "Please call me directly, Zach, at the office number, and I will make this right." A named person with a direct line is the most reassuring thing a reader can see.
  5. Stop. No rebuttal, no history, no "as we explained at the time." You will not win the argument in public and you do not need to; the reader has already decided you are reasonable.

When the review is unfair, the discipline is harder and more valuable. A short, gracious reply under an obviously unreasonable review makes the reviewer look unreasonable without you saying so. A heated reply makes you both look bad. We have watched businesses lose map pack positions in competitive packs after a string of combative replies, not because Google penalized the tone but because click-through and calls dropped as prospects read the thread and moved on.

Fake and suspicious reviews

Fake reviews come in three flavors: a competitor or a stranger with no record of being a customer, a disgruntled ex-employee writing as a customer, and the occasional mistaken review meant for a different business. Google's policy prohibits all of them, and you can report any review from the profile. Reporting is slow and inconsistent, so reply as well.

The reply to a suspected fake is short and factual: "We have no record of a customer by this name or of this visit. If we have missed something, please call the office so we can look into it." That tells the reader what happened without accusing anyone, and it leaves a paper trail if the review is later escalated. Do not write "this review is fake." You may be wrong, and if you are right, you have still told every reader that fake reviews happen to you.

The reviews you never answered

Most profiles have a backlog: dozens of reviews, some years old, with no reply. Answering them is worth doing, and the order matters. Start with the negatives from the last twelve months, because those are the ones prospects find when they sort by lowest rating. Then the most recent positives. Then work backward. A profile that goes from zero replies to all replies over a month looks, to Google and to readers, like a business that woke up.

Old replies should not pretend to be fresh. "Catching up on reviews we missed and did not want to leave this one unanswered. Thank you, Dana. The point about the parking is a fair one and we added directions to the confirmation email." That is honest, specific, and it still gives the next reader something.

A weekly routine that takes twenty minutes

  • Monday morning, 10 minutes: open the profile, read every new review, reply to each with the structure above. Flag anything that needs a phone call.
  • Same session, 5 minutes: send the review ask to last week's customers, with the specific question you want answered ("would you mention how the install went and how we left the space?").
  • Thursday, 5 minutes: check for replies you missed, reports that need follow-up, and any review that has escalated.

Twenty minutes a week keeps a profile current. Businesses that cannot find the twenty minutes hand it to us as part of Google Business Profile management, where every reply is written in the owner's voice and anything sensitive is routed to the owner before it posts.

Mistakes that cost rank and trust

The template. Fifty identical "Thank you for your feedback!" replies tell Google and the reader that nobody is home. Worse than no replies, because it is visibly automated.

Keyword stuffing. "Thanks for choosing the best Denver plumber for Denver plumbing!" Google's reply policies prohibit it and readers find it embarrassing. The city and the service belong in your profile fields, not in your thank-you notes.

Replying to a negative review while angry. Draft it, close the tab, read it the next morning. The reader cannot see the context; they can only see the tone.

Offering compensation in public. "We will refund you" in a reply invites a queue of refund requests. Move it offline, then do the right thing privately.

Disclosing anything about the customer. Appointment details, diagnoses, account status, what they paid. For medical, legal and financial businesses this can be a regulatory problem; for everyone else it is a trust problem.

Ignoring three- and four-star reviews. They are the most useful ones. The customer liked you and told you exactly what would have made it five. Thank them and say what you changed.

How replies connect to the map pack

Reviews are the most visible of the prominence signals in Google's three-factor local ranking model, and replies are the part of that signal a business controls directly. They do not move rank the way a category change does; they compound. A profile that gains four reviews a month, answers all of them, and asks for specifics in the ask tends to climb and hold. A profile that gains the same four and answers none tends to plateau. We see this in the monthly grid reports we run for our own companies in our Denver SEO work and for clients in every market we serve.

Questions readers ask

Should I respond to every Google review?

Yes. Every review, positive or negative, within two business days. Replies are read by future customers and are part of the engagement signal Google associates with an active, trusted business. Unanswered negative reviews are the ones prospects find first when they sort by lowest rating.

How do I respond to a bad Google review?

Acknowledge the experience without conceding facts you dispute, name the specific issue in neutral words, state what you did or will do, invite them to contact a named person directly, and stop. No argument, no history, no private details. The reply is written for the next reader, not the reviewer.

How do I respond to a fake Google review?

Reply briefly that you have no record of the customer or visit and invite them to call so you can look into it, then report the review through your profile. Do not call it fake in the reply; you may be wrong, and if you are right you have still told readers that fake reviews happen to you.

Do review responses help local SEO?

Indirectly and reliably. Reviews feed the prominence factor in local ranking, replies are evidence of an attended business, and a thread of calm, specific replies raises click-through and calls from the profile. AI assistants also read owner replies when deciding whom to recommend.

Can I use a template to reply to reviews?

Use a structure, not a template. Every reply should contain the reviewer's name, one detail from what they wrote and one true fact about how you work. The free builder in this article drafts that structure for you so each reply is specific without starting from a blank box.
