Bing Places and Apple Business Connect: The Listings Denver Businesses Forget

By Zach Wennstedt, founderPublished October 10, 20269 minute read

Almost every Denver business owner we meet has spent hours on their Google Business Profile. Very few have ever logged in to Bing Places or Apple Business Connect. Those two free dashboards feed Apple Maps, Siri, Microsoft Copilot and some of the search results AI assistants draw from. Here is what they are, why they matter more than they used to, and how to set both up in an afternoon.

The short version: claim your listing on Bing Places (you can import it from Google) and on Apple Business Connect (free with an Apple Account). Match the name, address, phone, hours and categories to your Google Business Profile exactly, add real photos, then put a reminder on your calendar to check both every quarter and whenever your hours or phone number change.

Why two more listings are worth an afternoon

For years the honest advice was simple: get Google right and the rest barely matters. Google still sends most local search traffic, and your Google Business Profile is still the listing that deserves the most care. What has changed is where people ask their questions. A growing share of “who should I call” moments now happen inside an AI assistant, a phone’s voice assistant or a car dashboard, and several of those surfaces do not read Google’s map at all.

Two examples matter for a Denver service business. When OpenAI launched search inside ChatGPT in 2024, it said results come from third-party search providers plus partner content, and Microsoft Bing has been reported as one of those providers. Microsoft’s own Copilot is built on Bing. On the other side, iPhone owners who tap an address, ask Siri for a nearby plumber or search inside Apple Maps are looking at Apple’s place data, which businesses manage through Apple Business Connect.

Neither of those facts means Bing or Apple will suddenly outrank Google for your business. They mean that if the listing those systems read is missing, stale or wrong, you are invisible or misrepresented in places where a customer may never see your Google profile at all. That is a cheap problem to fix.

What Bing Places controls

Bing Places for Business is Microsoft’s free dashboard for the business listings that appear in Bing search, Bing Maps and the Microsoft products built on top of them. It works much like a Google Business Profile: name, address or service area, phone, website, hours, categories, photos and a short description.

The practical difference is attention. Because so few local businesses maintain their Bing listing, Microsoft often fills the gaps itself from data aggregators, old directory entries and scraped pages. We regularly find Denver businesses whose Bing listing still shows a suite they left years ago, a former owner’s cell phone, or hours from before they stopped opening on Saturdays. When an assistant summarizes that listing, it repeats the error with confidence.

Bing also has its own category list. Some Google categories have no exact match, so an imported listing can land in a category that is close but not right. That matters because categories are one of the strongest clues any map uses to decide which searches you belong in.

What Apple Business Connect controls

Apple Business Connect, launched in January 2023, is Apple’s free tool for claiming and editing your place card: the panel that appears when someone finds your business in Apple Maps. Apple describes the same information flowing into Siri, Wallet, Messages and other Apple apps, so one set of edits shapes how you appear across the iPhone.

A place card can carry your logo, photos, hours, phone, website and action buttons such as booking or ordering. Apple also offers Showcases, short promotional cards for an offer or event. For most service businesses the basics are what count: correct pin location, correct hours, a working phone number and a few genuine photos of your team, trucks, storefront or finished work.

The Denver angle is simple. A large share of the people you are trying to reach carry iPhones, and Apple Maps is the default map on those phones. If your pin sits in the wrong spot on a Lowry or Central Park street grid, or your place card says “closed” on a day you are open, that customer will not dig any deeper.

Setting up Bing Places, step by step

  1. Sign in with an account you will keep. Use a business email that is not tied to one employee. Listings lost when a staffer leaves are a common, avoidable headache.
  2. Search for your existing listing first. Microsoft may already have one. Claim it rather than creating a duplicate.
  3. Consider the Google import. Bing Places offers an option to import locations from Google Business Profile. It is the fastest path, but treat the import as a first draft: review every field before you confirm.
  4. Fix the category. Pick the closest primary category from Bing’s own list and add secondary categories only where they truly describe the work.
  5. Complete verification. Depending on the business, Bing may verify by phone, email or a mailed PIN. Changes can take some time to appear after verification, so do not panic if nothing updates on day one.
  6. Add photos and a plain description. The description should say what you do and where, in the words a customer would use. Skip the keyword list.

Setting up Apple Business Connect, step by step

  1. Sign in at Apple Business Connect with an Apple Account. Again, use an account owned by the business rather than a personal one if you can.
  2. Search for your location and claim it. If your business is not in Apple Maps yet, you can add it.
  3. Verify ownership. Apple asks you to confirm you are authorized to manage the location. Follow the method it offers for your business.
  4. Check the pin. Zoom all the way in. Make sure the pin sits on your actual entrance, not the center of a shopping center parking lot or a neighbor’s roof. Service-area businesses should follow Apple’s options for businesses that do not serve customers at their address.
  5. Fill in hours, phone, website and categories, and add special hours for holidays before they happen.
  6. Upload a logo and real photos. Stock images undermine trust on a place card the same way they do on a website.

Keeping three maps in agreement

The single most important rule is consistency. Search engines and assistants cross-check your business across many sources. When Google, Bing and Apple all show the same name, address, phone number, hours and website, that agreement is a signal that the business is real and the details are current. When they disagree, systems have to guess, and guesses are where wrong hours and disconnected numbers come from. We explain the wider idea in our guide to entity signals.

A simple routine keeps the three in step:

  • Keep one master record. A short document with the exact business name, address formatting, main phone, website URL, hours and categories. Every listing copies from it.
  • Change all three on the same day. New phone number, new suite, new hours: update Google, Bing and Apple together, then your website footer and schema.
  • Use your main number. If you run call tracking, follow the setup in our call tracking guide so your main number stays consistent across listings.
  • Check quarterly. Open each listing every three months. Suggested edits from the public, and automated data feeds, can change details without telling you.

Once the big three agree, the same master record makes it easy to clean up secondary directories, including Colorado-focused ones such as COBusinessList, without introducing new variations of your name or number.

Mistakes we see in Denver listings

  • Old addresses after a move. Businesses that relocated from LoDo to the suburbs, or from a storefront to a home office, often updated Google and nothing else.
  • Seasonal hours that never changed back. Landscapers, snow removal and pool companies set winter or summer hours once and leave them for years.
  • Duplicate listings. One listing from the business, another created by an aggregator, sometimes a third from a former location. Duplicates split reviews and confuse every system that reads them.
  • Name stuffing. “Best Denver Plumber Emergency 24/7” as a business name violates the guidelines of every major map and invites suspension. Use your real name.
  • Pins on the wrong side of a highway. Along I-25 and I-70 frontage roads in particular, an automated pin can land somewhere a driver cannot actually reach.

How this fits your wider plan

None of this replaces the work that moves rankings most: a strong Google Business Profile, a fast and clear website, real reviews and content that answers customer questions. Bing Places and Apple Business Connect are the low-cost, high-certainty layer underneath. They take an afternoon to set up and a few minutes a quarter to maintain, and they make sure the answer a customer gets from Siri, Copilot or an AI assistant matches the one they would get from Google.

Questions readers ask

Is Bing Places free?

Yes. Bing Places for Business is free to use. You claim or create your listing, verify it and manage it from Microsoft’s dashboard.

Is Apple Business Connect free?

Yes. Apple describes Business Connect as a free tool for claiming and editing how your business appears in Apple Maps, Siri, Wallet and other Apple apps.

Can I import my Google Business Profile into Bing Places?

Bing Places offers an import from Google Business Profile. It saves time, but review every field afterward, especially categories, because Bing uses its own category list.

Do Bing and Apple listings affect my Google rankings?

Not directly. They matter because consistent details across major maps help systems trust your business information, and because Apple and Microsoft products, including assistants, read their own data.

How often should I check these listings?

Every quarter, and the same day you change your phone number, address, hours or website.
