Affordable is not the same as cheap

One Company Quotes $99. The Next Quotes $3,000. Both Promise Page One.

So which number is real? All of them — they just buy completely different things. Here is what each band actually delivers, including the one our own entry price sits in. We have been doing this in a competitive Denver market since 2012, and these numbers are what we see quoted every week.

Treat with suspicion

Under $200 a month, sold as a full campaign

The arithmetic does not work. At that price nobody can afford the hours a real campaign takes, so something has to be automated, outsourced to volume, or simply not done. The offer usually arrives as “$99 to start, then a few hundred a month” with first-page rankings promised.

What you might get

  • Automated directory submissions
  • A monthly PDF of rank positions
  • Occasionally a real listing aggregator

What is missing or dangerous

  • Guaranteed rankings — Google does not sell them
  • “100 links a month” means a link scheme
  • Private blog networks, which Google's spam policies target directly
  • No access to your own Analytics or Search Console

The honest alternative nobody tells you: in a low-competition local market, three to five hours a month of your own time using Search Console and your Google Business Profile will match or beat what this band delivers. If you are choosing between a $99 package and doing it yourself, do it yourself.

Legitimate, with limits

$200 to $500 a month

Real work happens here, but it is a template applied to your domain rather than a strategy built for your market. That is fine if you know what you are buying and worth knowing before you sign.

Reasonable to expect

  • Keyword rank tracking
  • Google Business Profile optimization
  • Five to ten citations or directory listings
  • Basic on-page recommendations
  • A monthly report with your actual Search Console data
  • Two to four blog posts toward the upper end

Not included at this price

  • Custom strategy for your market
  • Real link outreach
  • Meaningful technical fixes
  • Conversion work of any kind

The test: ask exactly how many strategist hours you are buying and what the link method is. A provider who cannot answer either question in a sentence is selling you the template and hoping you do not ask.

Where campaigns actually run

$500 to $3,000 a month

This is where a real campaign lives, and it is where most of our full-service clients sit. Published surveys put the average agency retainer around $3,200 a month, with roughly half of agencies quoting between $1,500 and $5,000 — so this band is at or below the market rather than above it.

What the money buys

  • Strategy built against your actual competitors
  • Technical fixes, not just recommendations
  • Content written for the questions you get asked
  • Earned links and local authority work
  • AI search visibility, which many agencies bill separately at $900+ a month
  • Conversion review — the part that turns rankings into calls

What it still cannot do

  • Guarantee a position or a date
  • Produce compounding results inside 90 days
  • Fix a business problem that is not a marketing problem

Timing to expect: positive return typically lands somewhere in months six to twelve, with the strongest results in years two and three. Anyone selling you a faster curve is selling you a risk.

📌 Short answer

Monthly SEO services are an ongoing engagement covering five connected areas: technical health, content and topical authority, local and entity signals, authority and corroboration, and conversion with measurement. Most local and regional engagements run $500–$3,000 per month, with single-location service businesses commonly landing between $800 and $1,500. The problem with how monthly SEO is usually sold

Ask three agencies what their monthly SEO service includes and you will get three lists that look broadly identical. Technical optimization. Content. Link building. Local SEO. Reporting.

Then three months in, the reports arrive. Impressions are up. Some keywords moved. There are graphs.

And the phone rings about as often as it did before.

This happens constantly, and it usually is not because the agency did nothing. It is because the activities were run in parallel rather than in sequence — five separate line items, each measured on its own, none of them feeding the others.

Traffic went up and revenue did not. That is not a mystery. That is what happens when the work is a package instead of a system.

Here is the practical difference. Publishing content on a site with indexation problems means writing things nobody will ever see. Building links to pages that do not convert raises the cost of every future visitor. Optimising a Google Business Profile while your business details conflict across the web feeds contradictions into the exact systems that decide whether to recommend you.

Each of those is a real activity, competently performed, producing nothing — because the layer it depended on was not in place first.

What "monthly SEO services" should actually mean

The categories of work are not really in dispute. Any credible provider covers roughly the same ground. What separates one engagement from another is three things:

  • Sequence. Whether the work is ordered so that each layer lowers the cost of the next, or performed in whatever order is convenient.
  • Depth. Whether "technical SEO" means an ongoing discipline or a one-time audit filed in month one and never revisited.
  • Scope honesty. Whether you were told in writing what is not included, before the work started rather than during month three.

Everything below is our attempt to be explicit about all three.

Why the order matters more than the list

Read the "FEEDS" line under each layer again. That is the entire argument for running this as a system.

Technical health makes content indexable. Content gives authority somewhere to point. Authority makes local signals more credible. Local and entity signals feed the corroboration AI assistants read when deciding whom to name. Conversion turns all of it into revenue that funds the next cycle.

Break the chain anywhere and the layers above it stop compounding. That is the mechanism behind traffic rising while leads stay flat — and it is why we will sometimes tell a prospective client that the honest first move is fixing something we do not bill for.

What the first 90 days look like

The most useful thing an agency can tell you before you sign is what month one actually produces. The honest answer is a baseline and a roadmap, not a traffic increase.

Weeks 1–3

Audit, baseline and roadmap

Technical audit, competitor and keyword landscape review, Google Business Profile assessment, and analytics and call tracking configured properly. A baseline is recorded before anything changes — without it, nothing that follows can be judged. The month ends with a prioritised roadmap.

Weeks 3–6

Technical repair and entity cleanup

Fix what is actively suppressing the site: crawl and indexation issues, speed, structured data, and conflicting business information across the web. This work rarely looks impressive in a report and frequently unlocks everything that follows.

Weeks 5–10

Content and local build

Publish content mapped to buying intent, build service and location pages with genuinely specific detail, optimise the Google Business Profile. Early ranking movement usually shows on lower-competition terms in this window.

Weeks 8–13

Authority and conversion

Earn links and third-party corroboration, then fix the conversion path so arriving traffic has somewhere to go. Conversion sits here deliberately — sending more traffic to a page that does not convert raises the cost of every future visit.

Month 4 onward

Compound and adjust

Rankings, leads and AI citations reviewed monthly against the week-one baseline. Priorities move as results come in. Meaningful organic results typically appear between months three and six, with substantial movement between six and twelve.

⚠️ On timelines

Anyone promising page one in thirty days is either misrepresenting the timeline or planning to rank you for your own business name and call it a result. SEO compounds, which means it is slow at the start and accelerates. That is a feature, and it is also why leaving is expensive once it is working.

Search and AI visibility run on one engine here

This is the part of the market that changed, and most retainers have not caught up.

People increasingly ask an assistant rather than run a search. Those assistants do not rank ten links — they assemble an answer and name a handful of businesses. Being named requires different things than ranking does: entity clarity, structured content, third-party corroboration, and facts specific enough to be quoted.

Here is what matters: those are the same foundations that make traditional SEO work. Which is why running them as two separate programmes is a mistake — it leaves the overlap unowned and weakens both.

Ask any agency you are considering: do you run search and AI optimization as one engine, or two separate streams? The answer tells you whether they understand what changed.

Nine questions to ask any SEO agency

Including us. If a provider cannot answer these plainly, that is the answer.

  1. Do you run search and AI optimization as one engine or two separate streams?They share entity clarity, structured content and corroboration. Split across separate scopes, the overlap goes unowned. Ours: one engine — layers 2, 3 and 4 do both jobs.
  2. What baseline will you set in the first 30 days?Without a recorded starting point, month six is unfalsifiable. Ours: rankings, traffic, leads, calls and AI mention rate, recorded before anything changes.
  3. What exactly is not included?The most useful question on this list, and the one least often asked. Get it in writing before work starts.
  4. Who is actually doing the work?Ask to meet them rather than the person selling. Ask whether any of it is outsourced, and where.
  5. How do you acquire links?If the answer involves a guaranteed monthly quantity at a fixed price, they are buying them — and the risk lands on your domain, not theirs.
  6. How is success measured?Leads, calls and revenue. If the proposed measure is impressions, sessions or "keywords ranked," you will get a campaign optimised for graphs.
  7. What does month one produce?The honest answer is a baseline and a roadmap. Anyone promising traffic in month one is describing something other than SEO.
  8. What are the terms, and what happens if I leave?Ask who owns the content, the accounts and the data. The answer should be you, in every case.
  9. What would make you tell me not to hire you?A provider who has never turned work away is a provider who diagnoses every problem as the one they sell.

Red flags worth walking away from

  • Guaranteed rankings. Nobody controls Google's results. Guarantees are either meaningless or describe ranking you for your own name.
  • A small price attached to a big promise. A modest budget aimed at one specific deliverable is honest. A modest budget claiming to cover audit, content, links and reporting all at once is arithmetic that does not work — ask which of those is actually getting hours.
  • Secret methods. Legitimate SEO is not proprietary. If they will not explain what they do, it is because explaining it would lose the sale.
  • Reports without explanation. Graphs and no account of what work was performed is a warning, not a report.
  • No baseline. If nobody records where you started, nobody can be held to where you end up.
  • They cannot define AEO or GEO. In 2026 that is not a gap in vocabulary. It is a gap in the service.
