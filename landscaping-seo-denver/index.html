What is landscaping SEO?

Landscaping SEO is the work of getting a landscape or lawn care company found and chosen by the homeowners you actually want. It covers the Google map pack for landscaper near me, service pages for design-build, hardscape, irrigation and water-wise conversion, the review profile a homeowner reads before calling, and the AI answers that increasingly summarize local options first. One company, two businesses

Mowing and design-build are not the same trade

Plenty of companies do both, and that is exactly when the marketing goes wrong. The two customers want opposite things from a website, and a site built for one quietly disqualifies you for the other.

Maintenance

Decided in four minutes

Someone wants a price for weekly mowing. They compare three companies on proximity and monthly figure, and the decision is essentially a commodity purchase. There is real money in it, but there is no version of that page where you win on anything except being cheap or being close.

Design-build

Decided over three weeks

Someone has been staring at photos of patios since February. They are spending five figures, they are frightened of getting it wrong, and price is maybe the fourth thing they weigh. They want to see work like the thing they are imagining, and understand what the process will feel like.

The mistake

Selling the second with the first's website

A homepage that leads with free estimates and weekly rates tells the project customer they are in the wrong place before they ever reach your portfolio. Same company, same crews, same quality — and a signal that sorts you into the cheaper pile automatically.

None of this means dropping maintenance. It means the two need separate pages, separate photography and separate language, so each one can be the best answer to its own search. Worth the hour: finding the one constraint holding you back.

An opening almost nobody has taken

The word for water-wise landscaping just changed

Here is a piece of history worth knowing if you sell in a dry climate. The word xeriscape was not a national trend that drifted west — it was invented here. Denver Water coined it in 1981, combining “landscape” with the Greek xeros, meaning dry, as part of a water conservation push after the droughts of the late seventies.

And in 2024 Denver Water stopped using it. They now publish under ColoradoScaping, because too many homeowners had come to equate xeriscape with gravel and cactus, which was never what it meant.

That leaves a genuinely uncontested gap. Homeowners still search the old word. Utilities and professionals increasingly publish the new one. Most landscape company websites use neither properly, and a page that covers the concept under both names — plus the plain-language versions people actually type, like low water yard and lawn replacement — picks up searches nobody else is answering. It is the single clearest content opportunity in this trade right now, and it will not stay open.

If you want to build that content properly, the source material is free and none of it is a competitor: Denver Water publishes ready-made water-wise garden plans, Colorado State University Extension maintains fact sheets like its guide to xeric ground covers, and PlantTalk Colorado runs a whole research-based library on the subject. Cite them, build on them, and your pages inherit some of that credibility.

Local demand

Four things that shape landscape search in a dry climate

All four are predictable, which means all four can be planned for rather than reacted to.

The February window

Project research begins while there is still snow on the ground. Homeowners spend late winter looking at photos and deciding who to call in March. Rankings that appear in April arrive after the decision was made.

Water restrictions and rebates

Utility incentives for turf replacement create a steady, policy-driven stream of searches with real budget attached, and they spike whenever a restriction is announced. Very few companies build a page for it.

Hail and storm recovery

A single severe storm shreds plantings across a whole metro and produces a wave of replacement and repair searches within days. Whoever already ranks catches it — see seasonal search demand.

The radius problem

Crews and trailers make distance expensive, so a metro is really a dozen separate local contests and the winner is whoever owns their own few square miles — more in how a metro splits up.
