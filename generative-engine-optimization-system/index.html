Generative Engine Optimization: Get Your Business Cited by ChatGPT and Perplexity

Also called answer engine optimization. The goal is not a ranking — it is being the business the answer names.

When a buyer asks an assistant who to hire, it names two or three businesses. The rest are never seen. That decision isn't made by ranking — it's made by how the whole web describes you. Here's how the naming actually works, and what changes it.

The part that gets skipped

There Are Two Gates. Most Businesses Only Work on the Second.

Answer engines do not read the web live and improvise. They run a search, fetch a shortlist of pages, then decide which of those to quote. That is two separate gates, and failing the first one is silent — there is no error, no warning, no ranking drop. You simply never appear.

Gate one

Retrieval — can it fetch you?

Before anything else, the engine has to find a relevant page on your site and actually read it. This gate rewards plain, boring technical health.

  • AI crawlers not blocked in robots.txt
  • Content in the HTML, not assembled by JavaScript
  • A page that exists for the question being asked
  • Enough topical authority to make the shortlist at all

How this fails quietly: a crawler that is blocked, or a page whose text only appears after JavaScript runs, produces no error anywhere you would look. Your rankings are fine. Your analytics are fine. You are simply not in the candidate pool.

→

Gate two

Selection — will it quote you?

Among the pages it did fetch, the model picks a handful of passages clear and current enough to build an answer from. This is the gate everyone writes about.

  • A direct answer in the first sentence or two
  • Specific, checkable claims rather than adjectives
  • Freshness — cited content skews measurably newer
  • Corroboration: the same facts about you, elsewhere

Worth knowing: gate-one fixes can move citations within weeks, because engines re-crawl. Gate-two authority work compounds over months. Most businesses see citation movement inside one to three months of consistent effort.

Different engines weight the gates differently. Perplexity is citation-heavy and rewards clean, sourced, recent pages. Google AI Overviews lean on what already performs in Google search. ChatGPT blends live retrieval with what it learned in training, which favours brands with a broad, consistent footprint — and it carries roughly 70% of AI search usage, so that bias matters more than the others.

📌 Short answer

Generative engine optimization is the work of making an AI assistant confident enough in your business to name it. Traditional SEO competes for a position on a page. GEO competes to be one of the two or three brands mentioned inside the answer — and that depends far more on how the wider web describes you than on how your own website is written. Five things decide it: identity that agrees with itself everywhere, presence on sources other than your own domain, content structured so facts can be lifted cleanly, crawler access that actually permits AI bots, and earned third-party mentions.

The conversation you're not part of

Someone who used to open Google and compare ten options now asks an assistant for a recommendation and acts on the answer. The assistant names a few businesses. Everyone else is invisible — not outranked, just absent.

What makes this worth taking seriously isn't the volume, it's the quality. In May 2026, visits from AI sources to US retail sites converted 54% better than non-AI traffic, and AI-referred traffic has grown more than fourteenfold since October 2024, according to Adobe Analytics. That makes sense on reflection: someone arriving from an assistant has already read a summary, already seen your name recommended, and clicked anyway.

You don't lose these customers. You never meet them. There's no traffic drop to investigate, because the visit never happened.

You don't need to rank first

This is the finding that changes the calculation for most businesses.

An Ahrefs analysis of roughly four million AI Overview URLs, published in March 2026, found that about 62% of AI citations come from pages outside the top ten organic results. Assistants draw on authority, relevance and corroboration — not position alone.

For a business that has spent years failing to displace an entrenched competitor, that's a genuine opening rather than a consolation prize. The entrenched competitor's ranking advantage transfers here far less completely than they'd like.

💡 What that does and doesn't mean

It doesn't mean SEO stopped mattering — strong organic visibility still helps everywhere, and it's what Google's AI Overviews lean on most heavily. It means ranking is no longer the only door. A business with modest rankings but excellent entity consistency and real third-party coverage can be named ahead of a better-ranked competitor with neither.

The engines don't work the same way

Treating ChatGPT, Perplexity and Gemini as one target is the fastest way to stay invisible on at least one of them. They retrieve differently, weight differently, and respond to changes on completely different timescales.

The five signals that decide whether you get named

These aren't equally weighted and they aren't interchangeable. Weakness in any one puts a ceiling on the others.

01

Entity consistency

The cheapest fix, and usually the biggest

An assistant assembles its picture of your business from fragments scattered across dozens of sources. When those fragments disagree — a different address on an old directory, a different category on one profile, a different description of what you do — confidence drops, and a competitor whose data agrees with itself gets named instead.

Do this: write one paragraph describing your business and one primary category, then make every profile, listing and bio on the web say exactly that. Old listings included.
02

Multi-source presence

Where GEO diverges hardest from SEO

A brand that exists only on its own domain has exactly one source of evidence, and it's the least trusted kind — self-description. Assistants weight independent corroboration heavily. Directories, review platforms, industry associations, local press and genuine third-party coverage are what turn a claim into a verified fact.

Do this: count the places your business is described by someone who isn't you. If the answer is fewer than ten, that's the constraint.
03

Extractable content

Retrieval works on passages, not pages

These systems lift chunks, not documents. Content written as flowing prose with the answer three paragraphs down gives them nothing clean to take. Question-shaped headings with the answer stated immediately underneath are retrieved far more readily — and Perplexity in particular rewards direct query-to-heading matches.

Do this: turn your service pages into question-shaped blocks. If someone read only the first sentence under each heading, would they have the answer?
04

Technical access

The silent disqualifier

Plenty of sites block AI crawlers in robots.txt without anyone having decided to, then wonder why they're never cited. Others render their content only after JavaScript runs, which some retrieval systems never see. Neither failure produces a visible symptom — you simply don't appear.

Do this: check your robots.txt for GPTBot, PerplexityBot, ClaudeBot and Google-Extended, then view the page source and confirm your actual text is in the served HTML.
05

Earned media and original evidence

The slow one that compounds

Original information gets retrieved and reused. Restated summaries of other people's work do not — there's no reason to cite the tenth version of something. First-party data, real numbers from your own operation, and specific knowledge of your actual market are the most citable things you can publish, and the hardest for a competitor to copy.

Do this: publish something only you could know — your own pricing ranges, your own timelines, your own local observations.

⚠️ On llms.txt

You'll see this recommended aggressively. It's a plain-text file at your domain root describing what your site covers and how to refer to your brand. It is an emerging convention, not a standard, and no assistant is obliged to read it.

Add it — it costs almost nothing and reduces the chance of an assistant describing your business wrongly, which is more common than people expect. But anyone selling it as the thing that gets you cited is overselling a text file.

How we build it

None of it replaces the fundamentals. Answer engines mostly find candidate pages through ordinary search, so the search work underneath is an input to this, not an alternative to it — and if your local presence is thin, that is the cheaper fix to make first. GEO extends a foundation; it does not substitute for one.

Ordered deliberately. Step two does very little if step one hasn't happened, because you'd have nothing to measure against.

Step 1

Build the panel and record the baseline

Ten real buyer questions, run across the assistants, with the results written down: who was named, in what order, and who appeared alongside them. Everything afterward is measured against this.

This is also the step that tends to change the conversation with an owner, because it converts an abstract worry into a specific list of conversations they're missing from.

Step 2

Make your identity agree with itself

Audit every place the business is described and reconcile the contradictions — name, address, category, service descriptions, positioning language. Unglamorous, cheap, and frequently the single highest-return work available.

Step 3

Build presence beyond your own domain

Directories, review platforms, associations, local press, partnerships. The goal isn't link volume — it's the number of independent sources that describe you accurately.

Step 4

Restructure for extraction and open the doors

Question-shaped content with answers stated immediately, plus a technical pass on crawler permissions, server-rendered content, schema and llms.txt.

Step 5

Publish original evidence, then re-run the panel

First-party data and specific local knowledge, followed by the same ten prompts on a fixed schedule. Same questions, same engines, same interval — so the movement means something.

🕐 What to expect, by engine

Perplexity: days to weeks once a page is crawlable and well structured, because it retrieves live.

Gemini: tracks your Google visibility fairly closely, so it moves roughly when your organic position does.

ChatGPT: months. It leans on accumulated familiarity, and there is no shortcut to having been mentioned credibly in many places over time.

Anyone promising fast ChatGPT results is describing a mechanism that doesn't exist.

Nearly everything written about this is aimed at national B2B brands competing against publishers with enormous authority. If that's the picture you've formed, it's discouraging and it's not your situation.

When someone asks an assistant for a roofer in Thornton or a dentist in Cherry Creek, the pool of genuinely relevant sources is small — sometimes a few dozen businesses rather than a few thousand publishers. And the signals that decide it are exactly the ones a small operator can build: business information that agrees with itself, real reviews, local coverage, and content about the actual area rather than the metro name repeated.

Why the timing is unusual

The Citation Lists Are Still Moving

Between 40% and 60% of cited sources turn over month to month. The models are still actively re-weighting how they choose who to name, which means the lists have not settled into the kind of entrenched order that traditional search rankings hardened into years ago.

That is genuinely unusual, and it cuts both ways. A business that is cited today can drop out next month. A business that is invisible today can break in far faster than it could ever climb the blue links. The window is open because nothing has calcified yet — and that is a description of the current state, not a countdown clock we invented to rush you.

For scale: AI search engines now handle an estimated 12–18% of English-language informational queries, against under 2% a year earlier. ChatGPT serves roughly 200 million weekly users asking conversational questions; Perplexity processes around 100 million queries a month. The curve is steep enough that "wait and see" is itself a decision.
